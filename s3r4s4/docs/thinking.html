Connections to literature

Theory scan (2026-04-14)

Targeted theory-literature scan. Five families of models that might give the project a sharper welfare object or testable mechanism. The three papers read closely (Padilla–Pagano, Elul–Gottardi, Chatterjee–Corbae) were subsequently promoted to theory.md; what remains below is the runner-up catalog plus unsettled residuals.

1. Credit-reporting and information-removal theory

The closest question to ours. These models ask what role shared negative information plays in a credit market, and what happens when that information is deleted or expires.

  • Pagano, Jappelli (1993) — already in literature.md. Foundational model of voluntary information sharing among lenders under adverse selection. Predicts sharing reduces default and expands lending but can harm borrowers with long credit histories.
  • Padilla, Pagano (2000) "Sharing Default Information as a Borrower Discipline Device" — adds moral hazard. Sharing defaults disciplines borrowers via reputation; sharing all information softens the discipline. Key prediction: the optimal amount of information retained is intermediate. Speaks directly to why Brazil has a Serasa regime in the first place and why wrongful listings are costly — the discipline mechanism only works if listings are accurate.
  • Vercammen (1995) "Credit Bureau Policy and Sustainable Reputation Effects in Credit Markets" — explicit model of how credit bureau memory length shapes reputation incentives and default. Argues an infinitely long memory destroys repayment incentives because no one recovers; optimal memory is bounded.
  • Chatterjee, Corbae, Dempsey, Ríos-Rull (2020) "A Quantitative Theory of the Credit Score" — signal-jamming / reputation model where the credit score is a belief object updated by lenders. Gives an equilibrium welfare criterion for when deleting a signal is efficient. This is the cleanest benchmark to borrow for our welfare discussion.
  • Elul, Gottardi (2015) "Bankruptcy: Is It Enough to Forgive or Must We Also Forget?" — already in literature.md. Repeated moral hazard / adverse selection model of default retention horizons. Optimal flag removal trades off incentives against insurance.
  • Daphne Chen (2011) "On the welfare implications of restricting bankruptcy information" and Chen (2013) "Can Employer Credit Checks Create Poverty Traps?" — formal welfare analysis of restricting negative information flows in credit and labor markets. Poverty-trap result is directly relevant if we want to discuss feedback into employment.
  • Corbae, Chen (2018) "Employer Credit Checks: Poverty Traps versus Matching Efficiency" — follow-up formalization.
  • Kovrijnykh (2023) "Building Credit Histories" — recent theoretical work on how individuals strategically build reputational histories. Could discipline our mechanism story for the entry into entrepreneurship margin.
  • Immorlica et al. (2022) "Optimal Credit Scores Under Adverse Selection" — mechanism-design take on credit scoring.

Takeaway: the tightest theoretical benchmark for our welfare story is probably Chatterjee-Corbae 2020 plus Padilla-Pagano 2000. Both distinguish the discipline channel (sharing raises repayment incentives) from the pooling channel (sharing improves screening). Our wrongful-listing setting isolates the second channel — the listing is not about the borrower's type, so removal is an information correction rather than a discipline relaxation. That's a distinction worth pinning down.

2. Consumer bankruptcy / default equilibrium

These quantitative models compute equilibrium default, interest rates, and bankruptcy choices. Useful if we want counterfactual interest-rate or credit-supply responses, not just reduced-form LATE.

  • Chatterjee, Corbae, Nakajima, Ríos-Rull (2007) "A Quantitative Theory of Unsecured Consumer Credit with Risk of Default" — the canonical workhorse.
  • Livshits, MacGee, Tertilt (2007) "Consumer Bankruptcy: A Fresh Start" — same vintage; different modelling choices (discrete vs continuous bankruptcy, rising income).
  • Livshits (2011) "Costly Contracts and Consumer Credit" — incorporates contract rigidity.
  • Exler (2021) "Consumer Credit with Over-Optimistic Borrowers" — behavioral extension; may matter if wrongful listings correlate with borrower optimism (probably not, but flag).
  • Chatterjee (2011) "Dealing with Consumer Default: Bankruptcy vs Garnishment" — relevant for thinking about what the outside option is when a Serasa listing is wrongful.
  • Tertilt (2007) "Accounting for the rise in consumer bankruptcies" — aggregate-trend decomposition.

Takeaway: these are all "big model" papers. We probably don't need to take a structural stand, but one of them should be cited as the benchmark quantitative framework against which our reduced-form estimates should be interpreted.

3. Occupational choice with credit frictions

Central to the entrepreneurship outcome. If we want to say anything about why credit access matters for the decision to run a firm versus work for a wage, these are the models.

  • Evans, Jovanovic (1989) "An Estimated Model of Entrepreneurial Choice under Liquidity Constraints" — ur-text. Wealth threshold for entry.
  • Quadrini (2000) "Entrepreneurship, Saving, and Social Mobility".
  • Cagetti, De Nardi (2006) "Entrepreneurship, Frictions, and Wealth" — quantitative GE with financial frictions.
  • Buera (2009) "A Dynamic Model of Entrepreneurship with Borrowing Constraints".
  • Paulson, Townsend, Karaivanov (2006) "Distinguishing Limited Liability from Moral Hazard" — structural decomposition of the mechanism.
  • Buera, Kaboski, Shin (2015) "Entrepreneurship and Financial Frictions: A Macro-Development Perspective" — review + aggregate implications.
  • Moll (2014) "Productivity Losses from Financial Frictions: Can Self-Financing Undo Capital Misallocation?" and related — aggregate TFP implications.
  • Gine, Townsend (2004) "Evaluation of financial liberalization: general equilibrium model with constrained occupation choice" — Thailand GE application.
  • Bassetto et al. (2014) "Credit crunches and credit allocation in a model of entrepreneurship".
  • Elul (2005) "Personal Bankruptcy and Incentives in a Dynamic Model of Entrepreneurship" — most directly on the default-and-entry margin.

Takeaway: our entrepreneurship outcomes (active firms, plant opening/closure, MEI, Simples entry) are closest to the Cagetti-De Nardi / Buera family. Evans-Jovanovic is the canonical citation for the wealth-threshold story. Levine-Rubinstein (2018) "Selection into Entrepreneurship and Self-Employment" is the modern empirical counterpart.

4. Reputation / commitment in debt contracts

These are older but relevant if we want to discuss what Serasa listings do economically — they are reputation signals that sustain repayment in environments with limited commitment.

  • Diamond (1989) — reputation model of borrowing; canonical "good types pool with bad types" story.
  • Eaton, Gersovitz (1981) "Debt with Potential Repudiation" — sovereign debt analogue; reputation sustains repayment.
  • Bulow, Rogoff (1989) — critique of reputation-only sovereign debt; reputation alone can't sustain debt without additional enforcement. Relevant: Brazilian private debt needs both reputation (Serasa) and courts (enforcement).
  • Cole, Kehoe "Reviving Reputation Models of Debt" — reputation spillover across relationships. Directly relevant to why a single negative flag is costly.
  • Hart, Moore (1998) "Default and Renegotiation" — optimal debt-contract theory with limited commitment.
  • Albuquerque, Hopenhayn (2004) "Optimal Lending Contracts and Firm Dynamics" — also "Equilibrium Default with Limited Commitment". Workhorse for firm-dynamics-with-default stories.
  • Bolton, Scharfstein (1990) — predation model based on financial contracting; not directly relevant.

Takeaway: the reputation family is the right place to cite when arguing that a single listing has large effects because it spills over across lender relationships (Cole-Kehoe) and that formal enforcement complements reputation (Bulow-Rogoff). Probably useful for framing, not for a structural model we'd estimate.

5. Search-and-credit / poverty-trap mechanisms

These connect credit access to labor-market and occupational outcomes through search frictions. Useful specifically because our outcomes include firm entry and exit.

  • Herkenhoff et al. (2024) — already in literature.md. Search model with unused credit as a buffer.
  • Daphne Chen (2013) "Employer Credit Checks and Poverty Traps" — formal poverty-trap story from a credit flag.
  • Corbae, Chen (2018) "Employer Credit Checks: Poverty Traps versus Matching Efficiency" — welfare trade-off.
  • Lochner, Monge-Naranjo (2011) "Human Capital Formation with Endogenous Credit Constraints" — parallel story for schooling; good template for the quantitative argument structure.

Takeaway: the poverty-trap mechanism is the cleanest way to motivate persistent effects of a single wrongful listing. Chen / Corbae-Chen is the right citation family.

Deep reads (2026-04-14)

Working notes from reading the three priority papers. The settled outputs live in docs/theory.md; what follows is the longer-form reasoning that led there, kept as a record.

Padilla, Pagano (2000) "Sharing Default Information as a Borrower Discipline Device"

  • Two-period Bertrand model with risk-neutral banks A and B, MH + AS. Borrowers are high-ability (mass γ, success probability p(effort)) or low-ability (always default, no creditworthy projects). Effort chosen once pre-lending, non-contractible.
  • Three information regimes: (i) no sharing, (ii) sharing past defaults only ("black"), (iii) sharing types ("white"/complete).
  • Central result (Prop 11): sharing only defaults can raise equilibrium effort above the level sustainable under either no sharing or complete sharing. Complete sharing actually removes the discipline effect because default no longer carries a stigma — outside banks can tell defaulters apart from low types on characteristics rather than default history, so the disciplinary channel disappears. In some parameter regions, complete sharing kills the credit market entirely (Prop 8) while default-only sharing sustains it.
  • First-best implementation (Prop 12): share default information on all borrowers plus reveal the type of a randomly selected fraction q < 1 of high-ability borrowers. Pure black + pure white together fine-tune the discipline to first best. Neither extreme alone achieves it.
  • Discipline vs pooling, formally: the disciplinary effect Δ(p) is the wedge between the post-default interest rate (borrower pooled with low types) and the non-default rate (pooled with high types). This wedge is strictly positive when the bank discloses defaults, zero when banks share only types. Effort responds to Δ(p) at the margin.
  • Public vs private registries: the model also predicts that public credit registers should be more common in jurisdictions with weak contract enforcement, because courts must be able to verify disclosure for the equilibrium to hold. Default information is easier to verify than type information, which is why private bureaus almost always share black information but rarely white.
  • What this means for us: the entire Padilla-Pagano framework assumes listings are truthful signals of past default behavior. Δ(p) > 0 requires the listing to be an accurate signal. A wrongful listing — the borrower didn't actually default — is a false positive that carries the same pooling consequences as a true default, despite conveying no information about the borrower's type or effort. In Padilla-Pagano logic, wrongful listings dissipate the value of the disciplinary channel (they make the signal noisy and generate deadweight loss from falsely penalized high-ability types). Judicial correction of wrongful listings is therefore not a weakening of discipline — it is maintenance of the information-sharing system's signal-to-noise ratio. This is a clean framing hook for the paper: our finding estimates the cost of signal errors in a Padilla-Pagano world.

Elul, Gottardi (2015) "Bankruptcy: Is It Enough to Forgive or Must We Also Forget?"

  • Stationary overlapping-generations model with a continuum of risk-neutral entrepreneurs each period. Safe types (measure s₀, always succeed) and risky types (measure 1 − s₀, success probability π_h under high effort at utility cost c, π_l under low effort). Lenders are one-period-lived and compete Bertrand. History of past funded projects is recorded in a credit history; a forgetting policy erases each past failure with probability q ∈ [0, 1].
  • Equilibrium object: the credit score p ∈ [0, 1] is a lender's belief that the borrower is safe, conditional on history. Markov Perfect Equilibrium characterized by (a) a region of no financing (p < p_l), (b) an intermediate region where risky types mix between high and low effort (p ∈ [p_l, p_h]), and (c) a region where financing is available for all p > 0 and risky types always exert high effort.
  • Optimal forgetting as a function of effort cost c (Prop 3, 4, 5):
    • High c (low marginal incentive from effort): no forgetting is optimal. Erasing failures only weakens the already-thin discipline.
    • Intermediate c: interior optimum. Forgetting improves welfare because it gives failed risky types a second chance to be financed — improving their reputation ex-post and strengthening their incentive to exert high effort in the new period. The negative effect (weaker ex-ante punishment) is offset by the positive effect (more periods of high-effort financing).
    • Low c: forgetting can be optimal up to q → 1. Low incentive problems → maximal value from giving everyone a fresh start.
  • Cross-country empirical match: countries with statutory forgetting clauses have higher private credit/GDP. Musto (2004) finds that US individuals whose bankruptcy flag is erased get more credit and subsequently default more than those with similar scores — the model's direct prediction.
  • Voluntariness: no lender would ever voluntarily ignore information it has. Forgetting must be government-mandated or enforced through a credit-bureau contract (Lemma 1 equilibrium logic).
  • What this means for us: Elul-Gottardi study ex-ante statutory forgetting — a blanket erasure rule applied after a fixed horizon to all bankruptcies. The Serasa setting is different in two ways.
    • First, our forgetting is ex-post and case-specific: each removal is adjudicated individually, and the court decides whether the listing was wrongful.
    • Second, the sample we identify effects on is not the universe of listed people — it is the subset who (a) had a plausible enough grievance to file suit and (b) were randomly assigned to a vara whose leniency tipped the decision.
    • In Elul-Gottardi's language, this is conditional forgetting — forgetting targeted at listings most likely to be errors. Their model says statutory forgetting is optimal in some parameter regions; ours studies an arguably better-targeted version of the same policy instrument. The counterfactual welfare object is: "what is the implied welfare of replacing statutory erasure with case-by-case judicial removal?" We can't answer this structurally, but we can cite their optimal-q result as the benchmark their model delivers when q is not conditioned on the identity of the listing.
    • Their Remark 4 is also relevant: if risky types can only fail with positive probability when low effort is exerted, there is no social benefit from forgetting because all failures are informative. The value of forgetting comes entirely from pooling high-effort failures with non-failures. Wrongful Serasa listings map onto this cleanly: the listing is a "failure" that was generated by a cause other than the borrower's effort/type — it was created by a creditor reporting error, identity theft, a time-barred debt, or clerical mix-up. These are exactly the failures that Elul-Gottardi's framework says should be forgotten, because they convey no information about type or effort. Our identification isolates this class of listings.

Chatterjee, Corbae, Dempsey, Ríos-Rull (2020) "A Quantitative Theory of the Credit Score"

  • Quantitative, heterogeneous-agent, unsecured-credit, long-horizon model. Hidden individual characteristic: patience β, which follows a persistent Markov process. Observable: earnings class, assets, default choices. Three additional state objects: a transitory earnings component, an action-specific extreme-value preference shock, and the market's type score s — a probability vector over β.
  • Central object: the type score is updated by Bayes' rule from observed actions. A default lowers s. Under weak conditions, a credit score (an ordinal ranking in [0, 1]) implements the same allocation as the type score — this is the equivalence theorem (Thm 3). Credit scores are a behavioral data representation of a reputation object.
  • Dynamic reputation as the unique default punishment: there is no exogenous exclusion and no monetary cost of default beyond a small administrative fee. The only consequence of default is that future lenders update s downward, leading to worse future borrowing terms. Yet this is enough to sustain competitive equilibrium lending with non-trivial interest rates and endogenous default rates.
  • Key welfare result (central counterfactual in §6): if lenders are prohibited from tracking individual credit history (they can still condition on age/earnings but not on past actions), the benefits of "bankruptcy forgiveness" — pooling of impatient young types with patient ones — are outweighed by the higher interest rates associated with lower incentives to repay. In this counterfactual, all young adults are made worse off in welfare terms.
  • Estimation: about three-quarters of people are born impatient; they become patient over time at a slow rate. Patience differs by only ~3 percentage points across types, but this is enough to drive meaningful reputation dynamics because extreme-value shocks keep actions partially pooling rather than fully revealing.
  • What this means for us:
    • Chatterjee-Corbae's welfare counterfactual is the canonical "no tracking at all" regime — and they come out firmly against it. Flag removal in their framework reduces welfare because the pooling benefit is smaller than the discipline loss. That is the opposite of our framing.
    • The reconciliation is the same as for Padilla-Pagano: their counterfactual assumes the tracked history is truthful. In their model an entry on the credit score corresponds to a genuine action the borrower took. Removing a wrongful Serasa listing is not "turning off the tracking system"; it is "correcting a mis-recorded action." In Chatterjee-Corbae's setup, a wrongful listing would correspond to a scoring function ψ that updates s downward on something other than the borrower's chosen action. The welfare implication of correcting such a scoring error is distinct from — and in fact opposite in sign to — the welfare implication of eliminating tracking.
    • This gives us a precise conceptual hook: the Chatterjee-Corbae framework is the strongest counterargument to a naive "flag removal helps entrepreneurs" story. We are not making that naive claim. We are making a narrower one: that correcting signal errors in a reputation regime of the Chatterjee-Corbae type raises welfare even though eliminating the regime would not. The paper could include a brief formal remark to that effect, cast in their notation — the Bayesian update after a wrongful listing violates their Lemma 1 consistency requirement (the type score stops being an unbiased posterior of the true underlying β), and restoring consistency is welfare-improving by construction.
    • This is a theoretical contribution we can claim without writing a full structural model: we observe an experiment in signal-error correction within a live reputation system, and we measure the marginal welfare return to that correction.

Synthesis: where the three papers leave us

All three models assume the observed credit history is truthful. None models what happens when it is wrong. That is the gap our paper can exploit.

Two concrete framing moves are now available:

  1. Discipline-vs-accuracy distinction (borrowed from Padilla-Pagano). The information-sharing literature has focused on whether to share defaults vs types, and on how much to share. Our paper adds a third axis: accuracy. Even holding the sharing regime fixed, the welfare value of the system depends on the fraction of entries that are actually true. Courts are the mechanism that polices accuracy.

  2. Error-correction as the welfare object (cast against Chatterjee-Corbae). We are not estimating the LATE of removing an accurate default flag — that's the counterfactual their model bounds. We are estimating the LATE of removing a false default flag, and that is a welfare-improving operation even under their assumptions because it restores Bayesian consistency of the type score.

Neither framing requires us to write or estimate a new model. They are reinterpretations of our reduced-form LATE against existing theory benchmarks. This is the most we can do without a structural component.

Whether the paper should include a two- or three-sentence formal remark in Padilla–Pagano notation showing that a wrongful listing generates the same pooling penalty as a true default without the associated signal value. Not yet in theory.md; flagged there under "not-yet-settled theoretical content".

What's missing

  • No theory paper I found maps directly to wrongful listings (information errors) as distinct from accurate-but-removed listings. This is a gap we may want to exploit in the framing. The closest is Padilla-Pagano's discipline-vs-pooling split, but even that models errors only implicitly.
  • No formal treatment of judicial delay as a friction in the information-removal process. The Ponticelli-Alencar (QJE 2016) enforcement-vs-credit channel is empirical; a pure theoretical analogue could be developed from Albuquerque-Hopenhayn-style limited-commitment with stochastic court resolution. This is a "could we write the theory ourselves" note, not a literature find.

Methodological sketches

  • Vara-level leniency instrument construction. Randomization is at the vara, not the judge, so the theoretically correct IV is vara leniency. Most foros have one judge per vara, so judge- and vara-level instruments coincide in practice for those cases. Where multiple judges have served in a vara, a weighted-judge-average leniency (weights = decision count, possibly computed over the judge's entire case history rather than just Serasa cases) should give the most powerful instrument. Also need to think about how to treat varas that change judge within the relevant window.

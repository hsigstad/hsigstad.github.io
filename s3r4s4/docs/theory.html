Theory

Theory

Settled theoretical apparatus that motivates the empirical design. None of the models here is our own; they are existing frameworks we position the paper against and test. Deeper exegesis, runner-up theories, and open conceptual questions live in docs/thinking.md.

Role in the paper

This project is empirical, not structural. theory.md documents the three information-sharing / reputation models whose predictions our reduced-form IV design can speak to. Each model delivers a different welfare claim about "removing a negative credit flag." None of them models the specific case we identify — wrongful (signal-error) flags corrected case-by-case through a court — so our empirical contribution is precisely the missing comparative static in a theoretical landscape that has otherwise focused on policy-level or statutory information removal.

1. Padilla–Pagano (2000): sharing defaults as a disciplinary device

Reference: Padilla and Pagano (2000), "Sharing Default Information as a Borrower Discipline Device," European Economic Review 44(10):1951–1980. File: padilla2000sharing.pdf (literature folder to be created).

Setup

  • Two-period banking-competition model. Two Bertrand-competing banks (A, B), continuum of risk-neutral entrepreneurs.
  • Types: high-ability (fraction γ, success probability p depending on effort) and low-ability (always default). Type is non-contractible; effort is non-contractible and chosen once, pre-lending.
  • Three information regimes the banks can commit to:
    • ns (no sharing): banks do not communicate at all.
    • is (information sharing on defaults only, "black information").
    • cs (complete sharing, "white information"): banks share types.

Core objects

  • Disciplinary effect Δ(p): wedge between the period-2 interest rate a defaulter pays and the rate a non-defaulter pays. Strictly positive under is, zero under cs.
  • First-best effort p̂ satisfies V′(p̂) = 2R*.

Main results relevant to us

  • Prop 11: information sharing about defaults raises equilibrium effort above both ns and cs. Complete sharing dissipates the disciplinary wedge by letting outside banks distinguish defaulters from low types on characteristics other than default history.
  • Prop 8: cs can kill the credit market in regions where ns and is support positive lending, because dissipated informational rents make the period-1 break-even constraint fail.
  • Prop 12: first-best is achievable by combining is with a probabilistic reveal of types for a fraction q < 1 of high-ability borrowers ("fine-tuning").
  • Verifiability argument: default information is easier for courts to verify than type information, which is why private bureaus almost always share black information but rarely white.

Testable implications for our design

  • The model generates a sharp prediction about the welfare cost of inaccuracy in the shared information: if a high-ability borrower's default record is a false positive, she pays the penalty rate R̄/µ(H|D)·p without ever having defaulted, which transfers rents to banks and distorts her effort-pricing calculus. Judicial correction of wrongful listings restores the posterior µ(H|D̄) for her and eliminates the deadweight transfer.
  • Prediction our LATE tests: among plaintiffs who sue to have a wrongful listing corrected, the ones who win should see (a) lower posterior default probabilities in lender beliefs, implementable as lower interest rates, (b) higher ex-post borrowing, and (c) higher effort-intensity outcomes (firm activity, entry into entrepreneurship). These are heterogeneous effects in p along the model's effort margin.
  • Prediction we do not test: we cannot identify the disciplinary channel itself, because our setting varies accuracy holding the sharing regime fixed. Δ(p) is a model object, not a reduced-form quantity.

How we position against it

The paper should cite Padilla–Pagano as the formal statement that information sharing is welfare-improving via the disciplinary channel, and note that this welfare benefit requires listings to be accurate signals. Our contribution adds the missing comparative static: the welfare return to accuracy maintenance in a Padilla–Pagano regime.

2. Elul–Gottardi (2015): forgetting past defaults

Reference: Elul and Gottardi (2015), "Bankruptcy: Is It Enough to Forgive or Must We Also Forget?" American Economic Journal: Microeconomics 7(4):294–338. File: elul2015bankruptcy.pdf.

Setup

  • Stationary overlapping-generations model. Each period a continuum of entrepreneurs is born; fraction s₀ are safe (always succeed), fraction 1 − s₀ are risky (success probability π_h ∈ (0,1) under high effort at utility cost c, π_l ∈ (0, π_h) under low effort).
  • Lenders are one-period-lived and competitive; they observe a borrower's credit history σ_t, a sequence of past successes S and non-forgotten failures F.
  • Forgetting policy q ∈ [0, 1]: each realized failure is erased from the credit history with probability q, in which case the borrower is pooled with same-history entrepreneurs whose projects did not fail.
  • Equilibrium concept: symmetric stationary Markov Perfect Equilibrium in which strategies depend on history only through the credit score p = P(safe | history).

Core objects

  • Credit score p. Financing thresholds p_NF ≤ p_l ≤ p_m ≤ p_h depending on the effort cost c.
  • Social surplus G = π_h R − 1 − c > 0 from financing a high-effort risky entrepreneur, B = π_l R − 1 < 0 from a low-effort one.

Main results relevant to us

  • Prop 1: MPE exists and has three regions in c. High c → single region where risky exert low effort, financing iff p ≥ p_NF. Intermediate c → all three regions. Low c → financing for all p > 0 with high effort.
  • Prop 3: no forgetting is optimal when c is high (incentive problem severe). Some forgetting is strictly optimal when c is low (incentive problem mild), because forgetting gives failed risky types an extra period of high-effort financing.
  • Prop 4, 5: for intermediate c, forgetting can still be optimal depending on s₀, patience β, and the magnitudes of G and B. Full forgetting (q → 1) is optimal when the risky share is small enough that pooling with the next generation is valuable.
  • Lemma 1: in equilibrium, no lender voluntarily ignores available information. Forgetting must be mandated by government regulation.
  • Empirical anchor (their Fig. 1): countries with statutory forgetting clauses have higher private credit / GDP.

Testable implications for our design

  • The model's welfare object is the probability q of erasing a failure, applied uniformly to all histories. Our setting replaces this with targeted erasure: a court decides case by case whether the flag was wrongful. If the model's forgetting parameter were interpreted as conditional — erase only failures not actually caused by low effort or risky type — the welfare comparison is strictly more favorable to forgetting than their baseline.
  • Remark 4 in Elul–Gottardi is the bridge: "when π_h = 1, forgetting failures provides no social benefit because failures can only arise from low effort." The contrapositive for us: when failures can arise from sources orthogonal to the borrower's type and effort — creditor error, identity theft, time-barred debts — forgetting those specific failures is welfare-improving independent of the incentive-weakening effect Elul–Gottardi emphasize.
  • Our IV delivers a LATE of targeted forgetting for the compliers: plaintiffs whose case was correct enough to sue and whose vara-assignment tipped the outcome. In Elul–Gottardi's parameter space, this is closest to a q_conditional experiment.

How we position against it

Cite as the reference theory on optimal forgetting horizons. State that their model characterizes the optimum for uniform statutory forgetting, while we study empirically the marginal value of conditional (judicial, case-by-case) forgetting in a setting where statutory uniform forgetting does not exist.

3. Chatterjee, Corbae, Dempsey, Ríos-Rull (2020): credit scores as reputation

Reference: Chatterjee, Corbae, Dempsey, Ríos-Rull (2020), "A Quantitative Theory of the Credit Score," NBER WP 27671. File: chatterjee2020quantitative.pdf.

Setup

  • Infinite-horizon quantitative model. Unit measure of individuals, exogenous mortality ρ. State: transitory and persistent earnings (e_t, z_t), assets a_t, and a hidden preference type β_t ∈ B (patience), drawn from a persistent Markov process.
  • Observable to lenders: earnings class, assets, default choices. Hidden: β_t and ε_t (extreme-value preference shocks).
  • Credit market actions: save or borrow in a discrete asset grid; if in debt, default or repay. Static default cost κ > 0 (filing fee). No exogenous exclusion, no stigma.
  • Type score s_t ∈ [0,1]^B: lender's Bayesian posterior over β_t given observables.

Core objects

  • Value function V(ε, β, z, ω | f) where ω = (e, a, s) is the publicly observed state.
  • Pricing function q^(0,a′)(ω | f) = ρ · p^(0,a′)(ω | f) / (1 + r), where p(ω|f) is the lender's probability-of-repayment assessment.
  • Type-score update ψ^(d,a′)(ω | f): Bayes' rule applied to the joint distribution of actions given types.
  • Credit score m = p^(0,ā)(e, a, s): the posterior probability of repaying a standardized debt.

Main results relevant to us

  • Thm 2: a stationary recursive competitive equilibrium exists.
  • Thm 3 (equivalence): under a regularity condition on the mapping m = p^(0,ā)(e, a, M(e, a, m)), the type-score economy is behaviorally equivalent to a credit-score economy. Lenders need only track the credit score, not the underlying type score, to implement the same allocation. This justifies interpreting actual FICO-style scores as market assessments of hidden type.
  • Default punishment is purely reputational: the only consequence of default is that the type score falls, leading to worse future borrowing terms. Yet this alone is enough to sustain a nontrivial default–repayment equilibrium with realistic interest-rate spreads.
  • Key counterfactual (their §6): if lenders are prohibited from tracking individual credit histories (they can still use age and earnings), the benefits of "bankruptcy forgiveness" — pooling of impatient young types with patient ones — are outweighed by the higher interest rates arising from weaker repayment incentives. All young adults are made worse off.
  • Estimation: about three-quarters of newborns are impatient, ~half of people are patient by age 60, patience differs by ~3 p.p. across types. Enough to drive meaningful reputation dynamics through the extreme-value preference shocks.

Testable implications for our design

  • The Chatterjee–Corbae counterfactual is the strongest theoretical argument against flag removal. If taken at face value, it predicts that Serasa removals should raise interest rates for un-listed borrowers (worse pooling) and reduce credit provision in equilibrium.
  • But the counterfactual assumes the tracked history is truthful — ψ is a consistent Bayesian update of the true underlying type. A wrongful listing violates this: the update is conditioned on an event the borrower did not generate. The observable ω then no longer carries the true posterior of β, and the equilibrium loses the welfare properties the theorem establishes.
  • Prediction our LATE tests: judicial removal of a wrongful listing restores Bayesian consistency of the type score for the plaintiff. The model's welfare machinery predicts a positive effect on the corrected individual's credit terms and borrowing, with ambiguous but likely small aggregate spillover (because the volume of wrongful listings is small relative to the population of tracked histories).
  • What we cannot test: the structural patience parameter, the persistence of β, or the full welfare integral. Our identification is local.

How we position against it

Cite as the canonical "tracking is welfare-improving" result. State that the Chatterjee–Corbae counterfactual studies removal of accurate history, while our empirical design identifies the welfare value of correcting inaccurate history within an otherwise intact reputation regime. The two results are complements, not substitutes.

Assumptions inherited from theory

  • We assume the Serasa system's listings are interpretable as "default events" in the Padilla–Pagano / Chatterjee–Corbae sense — i.e., lenders treat them as truthful signals of past default and update accordingly. This justifies treating a judicial removal as an update to the lender's posterior belief about the plaintiff's repayment type.
  • We assume random assignment of cases to varas (within foro-year) is equivalent to a random draw from the distribution of possible correction decisions, so that vara leniency is a valid instrument for the true correction outcome.
  • We assume BCB credit-registry variables measure the economic objects that Padilla–Pagano's rates and Chatterjee–Corbae's pricing functions refer to. Specifically: FOC_PC_TAX_EFT ≈ R_j in Padilla–Pagano; FOC_VL_A_VENCER ≈ the quantity of credit extended; FOC_VL_PREJUIZO ≈ realized default.

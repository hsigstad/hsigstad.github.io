Hypotheses — Theory-Evidence Scorecard

Role: Structured mapping of theoretical frameworks to existing evidence and testable predictions. Each hypothesis links one or more theories from theory.md to prior quantitative evidence and specifies what the vara-leniency IV design can adjudicate. Cross-references data.md for data sources and literature.md for cited work.

Status: Initial draft 2026-04-14, derived from the three deep-read theory papers (Padilla–Pagano 2000, Elul–Gottardi 2015, Chatterjee–Corbae–Dempsey–Ríos-Rull 2020) and the empirical literature in literature.md.

How to read this document

Each hypothesis entry contains:

  • Theory: Which framework(s) from theory.md generate the prediction.
  • Prediction: What the theory says should happen for compliers after a successful court-ordered Serasa removal.
  • Competing prediction: What a different framework predicts, where relevant.
  • Pre-existing evidence: What is already known from prior empirical work.
  • Evidence strength: How well existing evidence supports or contradicts the prediction.
  • Empirical test: How the vara-leniency IV design can adjudicate the hypothesis.
  • Data requirements: Which datasets are needed.
  • Priority: Based on (discriminating power) x (testability with our design) x (prior evidence leverage).

Evidence strength ratings:

  • Very strong: Multiple independent quantitative sources, consistent direction, in settings close to ours.
  • Strong: Quantitative evidence from at least one credible source in a related setting.
  • Moderate: Primarily qualitative, or quantitative but in a setting sufficiently different that extrapolation is contested.
  • Weak: Theoretically motivated but little direct empirical support.

Overall assessment

The empirical literature on credit-bureau flag removal is developed for contexts where the flag corresponds to an accurate record of past default — US Chapter 7/13 horizons (Dobbie et al. 2020, Musto 2004, Herkenhoff et al. 2021), the Chilean mass deletion (Liberman et al. 2018), and Swedish retention windows (Bos–Nakamura 2014). In those settings, theory and evidence agree that removal improves the corrected individual's borrowing terms and raises entrepreneurial entry on the gross-flows margin, while potentially imposing pooling costs on observationally similar non-defaulters (Liberman et al.'s equilibrium externality).

Our setting differs on one dimension that theory has not priced. The listings we observe being removed are adjudicated as wrongful: the court decides either that the debt did not exist, that it was time-barred, that the plaintiff was the victim of identity theft, or that the creditor failed to respect procedural requirements. In the language of our three theory papers (see theory.md), these are failures orthogonal to the borrower's type or effort — Elul–Gottardi's Remark 4 case, Padilla–Pagano's signal-noise case, and a Bayesian-consistency-violation in Chatterjee–Corbae. All three frameworks predict that correcting such failures is welfare-improving, even though none of them predicts that eliminating flag tracking altogether would be.

The paper's empirical contribution is therefore to estimate the LATE of targeted (case-by-case judicial) flag removal in a setting where statutory (uniform time-horizon) removal does not exist, and to compare that LATE against the predictions of these three frameworks.

The preliminary results (see docs/briefs/presentation_march2026.md) already support the headline prediction: IV coefficient of 0.104** on active-firm probability, a positive event-study profile starting at t = +1, and evidence of reduced firm closure. The BCB-side outcomes — credit stock, interest rates, guarantees, prejuizo — are not yet reported in the deck but exist in Ramon's pipeline outputs at bi-dropbox:AlencarAmoniMarianiSigstad/RA/Reports BCB/14042026/.

Tier 1: Strong prior evidence, central to the paper

H1. Successful court removal improves individual credit access

Theory: Padilla–Pagano (discipline-channel correction), Chatterjee–Corbae (Bayesian-consistency restoration), Elul–Gottardi (conditional fresh start).

Prediction: Compliers whose listing is judicially removed face lower interest rates, receive larger credit limits, and hold a larger stock of performing credit in subsequent periods, relative to observationally similar plaintiffs whose case was decided by a stricter vara.

Competing prediction: In a pure Chatterjee–Corbae world with accurate tracked history, forced removal would raise interest rates through weaker pooling (their §6 counterfactual). We argue this channel is absent here because the listing was a signal error: there was no legitimate information in it to begin with, so its removal does not pool a true defaulter with non-defaulters.

Pre-existing evidence:

  • Dobbie et al. (2020, JF): Chapter 7/13 bankruptcy flag removal raises credit scores by ~10 points, increases credit card and mortgage borrowing, with precise zero on employment and earnings. Magnitudes from the differential statutory horizons.
  • Musto (2004): post-bankruptcy flag removal raises credit access and subsequent default rates for affected individuals.
  • Liberman et al. (2018): Chile's 2012 mass deletion raised borrowing by defaulters by ~9%, but reduced aggregate lending by ~3.5% via pooling.
  • Ponticelli–Alencar (2016, QJE): court enforcement quality shapes firm-level credit supply in Brazil post-2005 bankruptcy reform.
  • Alencar (coauthor on this project) has direct visibility into the SCR side.

Evidence strength: Very strong on the direction of individual-level credit response to flag removal. No prior paper isolates the wrongful-listing margin.

Empirical test: BCB-side IV regressions of SCR outcomes (FOC_VL_A_VENCER, FOC_VL_LIMITE_CREDITO, FOC_PC_TAX_EFT, FOC_VL_PREJUIZO) on court outcome, instrumented by vara leniency. See docs/methods.md for the specification and docs/reference/bcb_reports_index.md for Ramon's existing 14042026 runs.

Data: SCR credit registry (via BCB environment, Ramon's pipeline) + TJSP court data for case outcomes and vara leniency.

Priority: Highest. This is the core BCB-side result the paper hinges on.

H2. Successful removal raises firm activity and entrepreneurship

Theory: Padilla–Pagano (restored effort incentive), Elul–Gottardi (extended financing window), plus the occupational-choice family (Evans–Jovanovic, Cagetti–De Nardi, Buera–Kaboski–Shin — see thinking.md).

Prediction: Compliers are more likely to operate an active firm, open new plants, and transition into SME entrepreneurship after a successful removal. Existing firms are less likely to close. The effect should be concentrated in the years following the court decision.

Competing prediction: Herkenhoff–Phillips–Cohen-Cole (2021, working paper) predict that flag removal raises gross flows into self-employment without changing the stock. Our baseline active-firm measure is a stock; the deck's event study on active-firm probability shows a stock-level effect, which is a sharper finding than Herkenhoff et al. would have predicted for US bankruptcy flag removal.

Pre-existing evidence:

  • Herkenhoff et al. (2021): flag removal → 12% higher borrowing among entrants, 4% more Schedule C income, 200% higher probability of employer-firm ownership relative to base rate. Stock of self-employment unchanged.
  • Herkenhoff et al. (2024, ResEcStud): greater unused credit lowers non-employment duration and raises match quality; elasticity ~0.6.
  • Chemin (2009): Pakistan access-to-justice programme raises entrepreneurship.
  • Lichand–Soares (2014): Brazilian juizados especiais raise entrepreneurship via access to justice.
  • Project deck (March 2026): IV coefficient 0.104** on active-firm probability, N = 97,732, weak-IV F = 309. Event study shows flat pre-trend and gradual rise from t = +1 to t = +5.
  • Project deck: IV estimates on n_plants (-0.052), new_firms (-0.121), closed_firms (-0.133), MEI (-0.086), Simples (-0.000). Interpretation in the deck: removal helps existing firms survive and avoid downgrading to MEI, rather than triggering new entry.

Evidence strength: Strong. The stock-level active-firm effect is the paper's headline pre-BCB result and is already estimated. The pattern (survival, fewer closures, less MEI downgrading, no new-firm effect) is consistent with Herkenhoff's flows-vs-stocks framework if interpreted as "the margin moving is the fate of existing firms, not new entry."

Empirical test: Already partially done. The next step is (a) re-estimate with the vara-level (rather than judge-level) instrument per methods.md; (b) decompose the stock effect into entry vs survival margins; (c) test Herkenhoff's gross-flows prediction by looking at transitions into/out of firm ownership separately from the net stock.

Data: CNPJ / Receita firm registry + TJSP case data. Ramon's do_name_mun_match.do + do_regression_by_cnpj.do already produce these outcomes.

Priority: Highest. Already in the deck; needs to be sharpened and connected to the flows-vs-stocks framing.

H3. Effects are driven by removal of listings orthogonal to borrower type

Theory: Elul–Gottardi Remark 4 (forgetting is socially optimal when failures are not caused by effort or type), Padilla–Pagano (removing signal noise restores the disciplinary wedge).

Prediction: The LATE should be larger in magnitude, and more robustly positive, on the subset of cases where the listing was clearly a signal error (identity theft, time-barred debt, paid-and-not-removed, clerical mix-up) than on cases where the plaintiff might have legitimately owed something.

Competing prediction: If the effect were driven by weakening of the discipline channel (Padilla–Pagano sharing-as-discipline result), then removals on any kind of listing would raise borrowing but at the cost of weaker repayment incentives in the population. The Serasa setting does not allow us to test this aggregate channel, only the individual-level channel.

Pre-existing evidence:

  • Project description of "inclusao indevida": the canonical causes of action in TJSP 6226 cases are exactly the orthogonal-to-type failures Elul–Gottardi Remark 4 describes.
  • Claims/ pipeline on Dropbox (bi-dropbox:AlencarAmoniMarianiSigstad/Claims/): regex-based classification of legal reasons in sentence text on a 1% serasa sample. Produces categories like time-barred, duplicate charge, identity theft, non-existent debt, procedural failure. Not yet joined to the main estimation sample.
  • No prior causal estimate separates "wrongful vs accurate" flag removal in any setting.

Evidence strength: Moderate. Theoretically predicted, but untested because no prior paper has access to case-level reason classification.

Empirical test: Heterogeneity by claim-reason category from the Claims/ classifier. Project the regex classifier onto the full estimation sample, then split the IV by reason category and compare LATEs. Expected pattern: clearly orthogonal failures (identity theft, duplicate charge, time-barred) deliver large positive LATEs; ambiguous cases (disputed amount, recent contract) deliver smaller or noisier LATEs.

Data: TJSP sentenca text + Claims/ classifier output + court outcomes + SCR/Receita downstream.

Priority: Very high. This is the test that sharpens the paper's theoretical contribution from "flag removal has effects" (already known) to "correcting signal errors has effects" (the gap Padilla–Pagano / Chatterjee–Corbae / Elul–Gottardi leave open). Requires lifting the Claims/ pipeline out of auxiliary status and integrating it into the main analysis — a significant workstream.

Tier 2: Secondary tests, still within core design

H4. Effects on interest rate should dominate effects on quantity for compliers with existing lender relationships

Theory: Padilla–Pagano (period-2 rate directly reflects the posterior µ(H|D̄) once the listing is removed; quantity is pinned down by zero-profit).

Prediction: For plaintiffs who already had an active lender relationship when the listing was created, the corrected posterior should primarily translate into rate improvements on existing credit lines rather than new-quantity expansion. For plaintiffs who were excluded from formal credit entirely, the effect should primarily be on quantity (the extensive margin of lending).

Competing prediction: Chatterjee–Corbae's framework implies both margins move proportionally with the credit score, with the interest rate being the more direct object.

Pre-existing evidence: None specifically. Dobbie et al. (2020) find both margins move.

Evidence strength: Weak. Prediction is theoretical, not empirically grounded.

Empirical test: Heterogeneity by pre-period credit-stock status. Split the sample by whether FOC_VL_A_VENCER > 0 in the year before the lawsuit. Compare LATE on rate vs quantity across the two subsamples.

Data: SCR with pre-period credit stock flag.

Priority: Medium. Clarifies the channel but is not central to the paper's headline.

H5. Heterogeneous effects by vara backlog / judicial delay

Theory: Elul–Gottardi's welfare argument depends on the discount factor β̃ and the probability of surviving to the next financing opportunity. Longer judicial delay reduces the present value of any eventual removal, which should attenuate the LATE for cases that took longer to resolve.

Prediction: Compliers in slower varas experience a smaller LATE (per unit of delay) than compliers in faster varas, holding the leniency instrument fixed. In the limit, a delay long enough to exceed the statute of limitations or the plaintiff's economic horizon would yield a zero LATE even for successful removals.

Competing prediction: If the relevant object is the stock of outstanding credit damage rather than the present value, delay might be approximately neutral — the court eventually resolves the signal error, and prior-period lender beliefs get updated at that point regardless of when.

Pre-existing evidence:

  • Ponticelli–Alencar (2016) show that court quality (including speed) shapes firm investment responses to legal change. Direct analogue for enforcement speed, not specifically for flag removal.
  • Chemin (2009), Lichand–Soares (2014) find that access-to-justice improvements raise entrepreneurship. These are reduced-form; they don't isolate the delay margin.

Evidence strength: Moderate. Intuitive but under-documented.

Empirical test: Two-dimensional heterogeneity on vara leniency × vara speed (median time-to-resolution). Interact the IV with a pre-period measure of vara delay. Alternatively, sample-split by case duration decile and estimate LATE in each.

Data: TJSP case data with filing and decision dates.

Priority: Medium-high. Directly speaks to the "judicial delay" part of the paper title and to the CPRI grant's motivation. Worth building early.

H6. Aggregate / spillover effects on non-plaintiff borrowers are small

Theory: Liberman et al. (2018) empirical pooling externality — deletion raised defaulters' borrowing but lowered aggregate borrowing by ~3.5% through worse screening. Chatterjee–Corbae's welfare counterfactual operates through the same channel at the full-population level.

Prediction: Because our LATE applies to a small subset of the SCR population (plaintiffs in TJSP 6226 who happen to be assigned to a vara at the margin of leniency), any Liberman-style equilibrium externality on non-plaintiff borrowers should be economically negligible. The paper should not need to model equilibrium spillovers.

Competing prediction: If there is a general-equilibrium response (e.g., lenders tighten standards in response to a high-profile win for plaintiffs), then individual LATEs would understate the social welfare gain.

Pre-existing evidence:

  • Liberman et al. (2018) document a 3.5% aggregate lending reduction in the Chilean mass-deletion context.
  • No evidence on spillovers from case-by-case judicial correction in any setting.

Evidence strength: Weak. The prediction is "small effects because small scale," which is untestable with our data.

Empirical test: We cannot credibly test this with the vara-leniency design. A partial check: compare pre-lawsuit credit terms of non-plaintiff SCR borrowers in comarcas with higher vs lower rates of successful 6226 removals. Interpret cautiously.

Data: Full SCR panel, all borrowers.

Priority: Low. Worth a one-paragraph robustness section, not a headline.

Tier 3: Predictions we are not well-positioned to test

H7. Effects on subsequent default probability

Theory: Chatterjee–Corbae predict that an individual whose type score is exogenously raised will subsequently show higher default rates than observationally similar non-affected borrowers, because the score no longer reflects their true type. Musto (2004) found exactly this pattern empirically for US Chapter 7.

Prediction: Compliers whose listing was removed should show lower subsequent default rates than Chatterjee–Corbae / Musto would predict, because in our setting the removal restores accuracy (they were never actually defaulters in the first place) rather than clearing a true bad signal.

Empirical test: Compare SCR FOC_VL_PREJUIZO (written-off credit) for compliers vs strict-vara counterfactuals in the 3-5 years after the court decision. Expect a negative (not positive) LATE.

Pre-existing evidence: Musto (2004) predicts positive post-removal default. Our setting should not replicate this if our interpretation is right.

Evidence strength: Moderate. A directly falsifiable prediction, but the effect sizes are likely small relative to noise.

Priority: Medium. Would be a clean test of the "wrongful vs accurate" distinction if the sample size is large enough. Requires careful pre-specification.

H8. Pre-trend diagnostics consistent with random-to-vara assignment

Theory: Identification assumption, not a prediction of any particular model.

Prediction: Plaintiffs assigned to more lenient varas should not differ in pre-lawsuit observables (credit stock, firm activity, comarca composition) from plaintiffs assigned to stricter varas.

Empirical test: Balance table on pre-period outcomes by vara-leniency quartile. Event-study pre-trends should be flat (the March deck already shows this for active-firm probability).

Pre-existing evidence: The random-assignment assumption is standard in Brazilian court settings (Lichand–Soares 2014, Ponticelli–Alencar 2016). Prior evidence supports it but does not specifically validate it for TJSP 6226 cases.

Priority: Required for publication, not a hypothesis that differentiates theories. Include as a robustness section.

What we are not testing (and should be explicit about)

  • Structural parameters of any of the three theory papers. We estimate a reduced-form LATE, not patience β, the disciplinary wedge Δ(p), or the optimal forgetting rate q.
  • Aggregate welfare counterfactuals. The Chatterjee–Corbae counterfactual requires a full structural estimation; we do not attempt it. The paper's welfare discussion will be qualitative and will borrow the three theory papers' welfare benchmarks as reference points rather than as targets to recover.
  • Occupational-choice mechanisms within the firm. Whether the active-firm effect is driven by wealth effects (Evans–Jovanovic), reduced precautionary saving, or lowered effective interest cost is not identifiable with our data. This stays in thinking.md.
  • Long-run effects beyond 5 years. Our event-study window is -5 to +5 relative to filing; effects beyond this horizon are not identified in the current sample and would require a larger case-year range.

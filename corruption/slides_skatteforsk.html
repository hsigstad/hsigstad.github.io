The trust problem in illegal exchange

Keangnam, Vietnam (2014): paid an intermediary $500,000 to bribe foreign officials for an $800M real estate deal. The intermediary stole the entire amount.

“There is truly no honor among thieves.”

— U.S. Attorney Preet Bharara

Corrupt agreements cannot be enforced in court. So how do they cooperate?

Keangnam (FCPA 654): Ban Ki-moon’s nephew and brother tried to bribe a Middle Eastern sovereign wealth fund official to buy Landmark 72 (Vietnam’s tallest building) for $800M. They hired Malcolm Harris — a fashion blogger with no actual connections — as intermediary. Harris simply pocketed the $500K and spent it on a Brooklyn penthouse and lavish personal expenses. Sentenced to 42 months. Bharara quote from the indictment press conference (DOJ SDNY, 2016). This sets up the question: how do corrupt parties solve this trust problem? This project

If trust is what makes corruption work, it is also where corruption is most vulnerable.

Theory: How can trust be established?

Repeated interaction

The cleanest example of repeated interaction sustaining corruption.

Metrô-SP (São Paulo metro construction): one construction firm, one government engineer, 0.5% of each monthly measurement certificate, every month for 17 years (1998-2015).

The cycle: government engineer approves how much work was done this month → Petrobras/state pays the firm → firm pays 0.5% to the political appointee who controls the directorate. Next month, same thing.

Why neither side defects: - Firm pays because: if they don’t, they lose access to the next contract. The engineer can block measurements, delay approvals, create problems. - Engineer delivers because: if they don’t approve measurements, the firm stops paying. The stream of kickbacks depends on the project continuing.

The folk theorem in its purest form: both sides prefer continuation to defection because the present value of future payments exceeds the one-time gain from cheating.

This is the TYPICAL pattern for infrastructure corruption — not the spectacular Odebrecht/Maiman schemes, but the quiet monthly cycle that runs for years without anyone defecting. “Nunca uma empreiteira tinha deixado de pagar” (never a construction firm had failed to pay) — Netto (2016) on the Petrobras cartel, same logic at larger scale.

At the Petrobras scale, this same bilateral repeated interaction operated across dozens of firms and three political parties simultaneously — each party controlling a directorate, each firm paying its percentage per project. The cartel coordinated WHO got which contract, but the TRUST on each bilateral link was the monthly repeated game.

Source: procure project court cases (TJSP sentenças). Netto (2016) for Petrobras parallel.

Friendship / kinship

Subtype of repeated interaction: when the corrupt parties are friends or relatives, the trust problem is already solved by the pre-existing personal bond.

Frank Murud, property manager at Undervisningsbygg (Oslo’s school renovation agency, 2006). Channeled contracts via fictitious invoices to his old carpentry colleague and cabin neighbor who had a firm. Had previously used the same method to tap his private employer NEAS.

90M NOK stolen by Murud and co-defendants. Accumulated: 17M NOK cabin, 36M in shares, 13M house, apartments in Lillehammer and Fredrikstad, expensive cars and boats. A supplier also delivered stereo equipment and a hot tub to Murud privately.

Caught when he cycled 20M NOK through his wife’s account over a December/January boundary, triggering tax reporting and a money laundering alert to Økokrim.

The friendship IS the trust mechanism: no need for formal agreements, intermediaries, or repeated testing. The decades-long personal bond (colleague + neighbor) made defection socially unthinkable — you don’t betray your cabin neighbor.

Norwegian context: “I Norge handler korrupsjon om nettverk og vennetjenester, ikke pengebeløp i skatteparadiser” (Espen Søbye, cited in Hjellum 2007). The vennetjeneste concept — corruption as mutual favors among friends — captures exactly this mechanism.

Compare Bærum kommune (same year): contracts directed to “nettverk av familiemedlemmer og venners selskaper” (network of family members’ and friends’ companies), 22M NOK. Same pattern.

Source: Gedde-Dahl et al. (2008) “Korrupsjon i Norge.”

Contractual formalization

Kongsberg Defence Communications (Norway, 50% state-owned) sold military communications equipment to Romania. 1999-2008. ~1.5B NOK ($180M) in contracts. Hired two retired Romanian generals as agents. Total fees: ~170M NOK ($20M).

The generals were on formal no-cure-no-pay agent agreements. “The agents were not paid for effort, it was the result that counted. All agent agreements were based on the principle of ‘no cure, no pay’. Only when the delivery was secured did the agent get money.” Up to 20% of contract value.

Sekkelsten (sales director): “To think that a Norwegian company could win contracts in Romania without using unethical methods is naive. It is not possible in such markets when you come from a small country.”

The second general “had sat on the other side of the table two years earlier, signing the first purchase from Kongsberg” — then registered a consulting company on Isle of Man. Sekkelsten: “Nobody knew STS better than him.”

Trust architecture: generals’ NETWORK (relational — decades in Romanian military/intelligence) was the access. The PAYMENT was contractual (formal success fee — generals could sue if not paid). Two mechanisms bundled.

Deliberate ignorance: “We know they share their earnings, but close our ears to that kind of knowledge.”

Source: DN documentary “Korrupsjonssaken som forsvant” (2021). TRACE Compendium case 606.

When agent fees are banned

Kongsberg banned large agent fees. Bribes moved to an inflated subcontract:

Omnilogic price: 65 → 100 MNOK — no change in deliverables.

“Software costs are best if you want to make changes. You can’t say whether it cost 100 or 200 million.”

Same Kongsberg/Romania case. After compliance tightened (~2004), Sekkelsten switched from agent commissions to inflating a legitimate subcontractor’s price.

Omnilogic was a Romanian software supplier already part of the delivery. Agent fees were hidden as inflated software costs — 65 MNOK became 100 MNOK (+35 MNOK, ~53% increase).

Sekkelsten’s logic: “The bribes had to find new routes. One could outsource parts of the delivery locally, to local suppliers. And pay a bit more for the service, in exchange for them paying a certain number of individuals.”

Key point for the talk: this is money laundering, not trust. The subcontract is signed AFTER the corrupt award — it creates a payment channel, not an incentive-alignment device. Contrast with the Kongsberg agent contract (signed before, success-fee aligned incentives) or the Airbus case (agent could sue if not paid).

Compliance red flags for subcontractors (from OECD/World Bank): overpricing, lack of experience, new firm, politically connected, refusal of audit rights.

These contracts are enforceable

Agent helped Airbus sell 30 Caracal helicopters to Kuwait for EUR 1B.

Airbus refused his commission. He sued — and won EUR 12M (ICC arbitration, 2020).

Abdelnour v Airbus (2020): the clearest proof that corrupt agent contracts are legally enforceable.

Farid Abdelnour helped Airbus secure a EUR 1 billion contract for 30 Caracal military helicopters to Kuwait (2016). When Airbus refused to pay his commission, he took them to ICC arbitration and won EUR 12 million in November 2020.

The arbitrator found that Airbus and Abdelnour had “carried out business as usual” — internal emails showed Airbus CEO corresponded with Abdelnour even while under bribery investigation. Airbus had told Kuwait no commissions were or would be paid. CEO Faury wrote “none” beside each of six bullet points requesting commission details.

Not an isolated case: Hilmarton v OTV (Algeria, won after 11 years), Westacre v Jugoimport ($50M+, Kuwait arms, enforced by English Court of Appeal), Bhandari v Thales (EUR 11M, India Mirage upgrade, pending — filed by a fugitive).

All are in lumpy, one-shot sectors (defense/arms) — exactly where the folk theorem predicts formal contracts are needed because there’s no repeat game to enforce payment.

Source: OCCRP (2024), “As Bribery Probe Unfolded, Airbus Kept in Touch With Middleman on Controversial Kuwait Helicopters Deal.”

Contractual formalization: judicial corruption

Same trust architecture as Kongsberg, different setting.

With random case assignment, the litigant doesn’t know which judge they’ll face — so they can’t build bilateral trust. The lawyer bridges the gap: - Litigant → Lawyer: contingency fee (contractual trust, incentive-aligned) - Lawyer → Judge: personal relationship (repeat interaction in court)

The lawyer has the same structural position as the Kongsberg generals: contractual trust on the client side, relational trust on the official side.

But there’s an important difference: if contingency fees are BANNED (as in some jurisdictions), model (a) doesn’t work. Then the litigant needs a RETAINER relationship with a lawyer who knows all judges — model (b). This shifts from contractual trust to something closer to Bernstein-style trust brokerage: the lawyer has repeat interaction with both the client (ongoing retainer) and the judges (appears before them regularly). The lawyer IS the trust broker.

This is possibly the one setting in corruption where Bernstein-style brokerage genuinely applies — because random assignment PREVENTS bilateral trust between litigant and judge, and the lawyer permanently stands between them.

Connection to the connect project: lawyer-judge connections in Brazilian labor courts. The observed pattern (certain lawyers disproportionately winning before certain judges) is consistent with both models. Distinguishing requires knowing the fee structure.

Prediction: jurisdictions banning contingent fees should show MORE relationship-based judicial corruption (model b); jurisdictions allowing them should show MORE market-based (model a, hire the connected lawyer for this specific judge).

Trust brokerage

Trust brokerage (Bernstein 1992): the broker has repeat interaction with BOTH sellers and buyers, bridging parties who interact too infrequently for bilateral trust. In the NY Diamond Dealers Club, photos of non-complying members are hung on the wall — community enforcement through public shaming.

Requirements for this to work: - Visible community (DDC members know each other) - Standardized transactions (graded diamonds, known prices) - Multi-client broker (serves many on each side) - No direct repeated interaction between the two sides

Key question for our project: does this model apply to corruption intermediaries?

In 6 detailed cases we examined (Toledo/Maiman, TSKJ/Tesler, Kongsberg/Romania, Oil-for-Food/Alia, Harris/Keangnam, Monsanto/Indonesia), the intermediary NEVER functions as a permanent trust broker in this sense. Instead: - Maiman introduced Barata to Toledo → they then met directly at the palace - Tesler coordinated 4 firms and handled cash logistics, but Stanley flew to Abuja and met officials directly - Kongsberg generals provided introductions, but Sekkelsten built his own flipover charts of 200+ officials

In every case, parties develop DIRECT repeated interaction through the project — milestones, addenda, inaugurations. The intermediary becomes redundant for trust.

Exception: judicial corruption with random case assignment (previous two slides). The lawyer permanently stands between litigant and judge because random assignment prevents bilateral trust.

Reference: Bernstein, Lisa (1992). “Opting Out of the Legal System: Extralegal Contractual Relations in the Diamond Industry.” J Legal Studies 21(1): 115-157.

Reputation mechanisms

Three reputation channels — all solve the trust problem, but through different information architectures.

LEFT — Darknet markets: Silk Road and successors use eBay-style ratings. Pseudonymous identity is persistent, so a seller’s username accumulates a public track record. This is Kandori (1992) community enforcement, digitized. Works because the information architecture allows it.

CENTER — Spear’s 500: curated ranking of offshore trust advisers. The service is LEGAL — setting up an offshore trust is perfectly legitimate. But the same advisers, trusts, and jurisdictions enable tax evasion. A potential evader can find a reliable enabler without ever discussing illegality.

RIGHT — Violence (Escobar): reputation for PUNISHMENT. Car bombs, narco-messages, signature killings — all deliberately visible. The community observes and infers: this person WILL punish defection. Gambetta (1993): violence is communication, not just punishment.

Key difference: darknet and Spear’s signal RELIABILITY (“I deliver good service”). Violence signals PUNISHMENT (“I will destroy you if you defect”). Corruption lacks both channels — officials can’t build public track records (secrecy) and generally can’t use visible violence (legitimate positions to protect).

Our data: reputation in ~89% of tax evasion cases, ~15% of drug trafficking, ~2% of corruption.

References: Christin (2013), Harrington (2016), Gambetta (1993, 2009).

When does each mechanism dominate?

Trust mechanism Predicted to dominate when…
Repeated interaction Long horizon, granular payments, stable positions
Trust broker Many buyers and sellers, pairs rarely repeat
Contractual formalization No repeated relation, legal contract can align incentives
Reputation Public track record possible, weak enforcement
Violence / coercion Weak state

This table summarizes our main hypotheses. Each row maps to a trust mechanism we just illustrated with cases.

Key hypotheses behind each row: - Repeated interaction (H20, H40): relational dominance rises with the discount factor. Granular payment splitting (monthly measurements, addenda) deliberately creates a repeated game. Sector lumpiness determines whether this is possible. - Intermediary (H2, H33): cross-border deals involve strangers from different legal systems, increasing demand for intermediaries. Compliance regimes select for professional enablers over simple fixers. - Friendship/kinship (H11): pre-existing relationships provide trust without needing repeated corrupt deals. Defection costs the friendship, not just future deals. - Contractual formalization (H32, H41): lumpy transactions (defense, telecom licenses) can’t sustain a repeated game. Success-fee contracts substitute for relational trust. All agent commission lawsuits are in lumpy sectors (defense/arms). - Reputation (H34): requires observable track record. Works in drug markets (pseudonymous ratings) and tax evasion (legal professional rankings), but corruption’s secrecy requirement destroys the information channel. - Violence/coercion: requires that the state cannot prosecute visible punishment. Politicians generally can’t use this channel (legitimate positions to protect).

Contractual trust

Prompt:

“Extract all contracts and agreements mentioned. Include arrangements implied by transactional language (‘commission fee’, ‘success fee’, ‘appointed as agent’).”

Case document (Tenaris / Uzbekistan):

“Tenaris agreed to pay the OAO agent approximately 3 to 3.5% of the awarded contracts in exchange for the agent’s services, knowing that all or a portion of the money would be given by the agent to one or more OAO employees.”

→ LLM extracts: contingent_fee: true, fee_structure: "3 to 3.5% of the awarded contracts", type: "agent_agreement"

In 17% of FCPA cases, the document describes a commission or fee tied to results.

Contractual trust: does the contract ALIGN INCENTIVES? A success-fee contract (30% commission on sales) gives the agent skin in the game — they only get paid if they deliver. This is genuine trust formalization.

The LLM extracts observables: contingent_fee (boolean), fee_structure (free text), contract type. No trust judgment. We then classify mechanically: if contingent_fee=true OR type=agent_agreement → contractual trust.

The Lindsey Manufacturing case is a clean example: 30% commission “significantly higher” than the previous rep — the premium signals the corrupt purpose while the contractual form aligns incentives.

Important: most contracts in corruption cases are NOT trust devices. They’re payment channels (sham invoices, cover contracts). Only ~17% of FCPA cases show genuine contractual trust formalization.

The 124 genuine contractual trust cases are concentrated in FCPA (97 cases, 12.6%) where agent/consultant arrangements are the standard vehicle.

Four presidents, four intermediaries

Summary slide: same firm (Odebrecht), same country director (Barata), four consecutive presidents — but a completely different intermediary and trust mechanism each time.

Toledo: cold approach via Israeli businessman Maiman, who was a personal friend of Toledo’s wife since 1972. Barata had to verify Maiman’s access by watching him enter the presidential palace through a side door.

García: no intermediary needed — three generations of Odebrecht CEOs had direct personal relationships with García spanning 20+ years. Operators (Nava, Cuba) handled logistics through APRA party networks.

Humala: access via Brazilian Workers’ Party (PT) network. João Santana provided the “full package” — campaign consulting + financing. Cash delivered through doleiros (money launderers).

PPK: technocratic channel. Westfield Capital was PPK’s own one-person consulting firm in Miami. Odebrecht paid $4.8M in “consulting fees” while PPK served as Economy Minister. PPK claimed a “Chinese wall” between his government role and private firm.

The variation across four consecutive presidents — in one country, by one firm — is the cleanest illustration of how the available trust technology shapes the organizational form of corruption.

Toledo (2001–06): the trust puzzle

Odebrecht and Toledo agree on a $35M bribe for the Interoceânica highway ($814M).

Toledo rigs the bidding. Odebrecht wins (June 2005).

But Toledo has a single 5-year term, no reelection, and is about to leave office.

Why did Toledo trust Odebrecht would pay the bribe?

Source: Gaspar (2020), Durand (2018), Barata plea testimony.

Nov 2004: Barata agrees to $35M at Marriott meeting in Copacabana — Toledo himself physically present. June 2005: bidding concluded, Odebrecht wins. Toledo leaves office end 2005/mid 2006.

No agency fee contract. Maiman (Israeli businessman, friend of Toledo’s wife) served as intermediary throughout — shield and payment channel, not trust broker. The trust problem is Toledo’s: he delivers first, then must wait for payment from a firm that no longer needs him.

The end-game problem

Bribe agreed: $35M. Bribe paid: $20M. Remaining $15M never paid.

“Toledo left the presidency and lost influence.” — Gaspar (2020)

Why did Odebrecht pay the $20M at all? Possible explanations:

  • Toledo still had power — $20M paid mid-2005, while he was still president and could obstruct construction permits
  • Odebrecht’s reputation — operated in 12 countries with intermediaries and politicians; stiffing Maiman could signal non-payment to others
  • Maiman useful elsewhere — Maiman had operations in Venezuela, Turkmenistan, Egypt; maintaining the relationship had option value

But $15M not paid undermines all three latter stories. Open question.

Source: Gaspar (2020), Durand (2018).

The timing matters: $20M was paid “in the following months” after the June 2005 bid — Toledo was still president until end 2005/mid 2006. He still controlled permits, BNDES disbursements, environmental approvals. Construction was just starting. So the simplest explanation for the $20M: pay the sitting president while he can still hurt you.

But then the $15M non-payment is the end-game in action. Maiman “collected aggressively” (exigían) but couldn’t enforce. Corrupt debts can’t be enforced in court.

The reputation story has a tension: if reputation mattered enough to pay $20M, why not the last $15M? Possible resolution: the $20M was paid while Toledo still had power (not purely reputation), and the $15M shortfall emerged only after power expired.

Contrast with Karimova (Uzbekistan) who demanded MORE — she still held power. Maiman received LESS — he’d lost his power source.

Toledo ran for president again in 2011 (lost). Some continued payments (2006-2010) may have been Odebrecht maintaining an option on his return.

How infrastructure corruption normally solves this

Rodoanel Sul highway (São Paulo, Odebrecht, 2006–07):

  • Governor forces 4% price reduction on Odebrecht
  • Contract allows construction technique changes → preserves Odebrecht’s margins
  • Bribe: 0.75% of each invoice paid to intermediary Paulo Preto
  • If a contract addendum is granted to increase the contract value, bribe rises to 2% of each invoice

Each addendum is a new round of exchange. Both sides deliver incrementally — neither needs to trust the other with a large one-shot payment.

Toledo couldn’t do this — single term, no repeat game. Result: $15M never paid.

Source: Gaspar (2020), chapter on São Paulo state contracts (Rodoanel Sul).

The intermediary Paulo Preto (Paulo Vieira de Souza) explained that the governor would force price reductions, but “os descontos podiam ser compensados depois com modificações nos contratos” — discounts could be compensated later through contract modifications.

The bribe escalation clause (0.75% → 2% if addendum granted) explicitly ties the corrupt payment to the contract amendment. This makes the addendum itself the trust device: Odebrecht gets more contract value, the official/intermediary gets a higher percentage, both delivered incrementally through invoices.

Odebrecht’s own calculation: by changing construction techniques, they saved ~70M reais. The commission was 2.2M reais. “Everyone came out ahead” — except the state treasury.

This is the general pattern: addenda, monthly measurements, per-shipment fees all convert a lumpy transaction into a repeated game. The folk theorem does the rest.

Toledo’s Interoceânica was different: single term, one project award, no mechanism to split into rounds. Result: $15M of $35M never paid.

Defense contracts are lumpy — one award, one delivery. Trust requires a formal contract (Kongsberg: commission per contract win; Airbus: success fee).

Infrastructure is granular — addenda, invoices, milestones. Trust builds through repeated interaction between the same people over years.

Source: FCPA trust_observables data. Defense = sector tagged “defense” or “military”. Infrastructure = “infrastructure”, “construction”, “energy”, “oil”, “mining”.

The “work colleagues” finding is striking: 77 of 137 infrastructure relationship pairs are coded as work colleagues — people who work together on projects over years. This IS repeated interaction. Defense has more kinship (6) and prior_business (7) — pre-existing trust that substitutes for the repeat game.

Ongoing business flag: 83% of defense relationships vs 64% of infrastructure. But this is among the minority of cases where relationships are described at all (27% defense, 20% infrastructure).

The agent agreement / contingent fee gap is the cleanest finding: defense relies on formal contractual trust 2-7x more than infrastructure. This maps to theory: the folk theorem works when transactions are granular; contractual formalization substitutes when they’re not.

One firm, three factions, three intermediaries

Same pattern as Peru but within one country. Odebrecht’s SOD used different intermediaries for each political faction:

  • PP: Youssef, a money launderer tied to PP congressman Janene since 1997. Cash conversion and delivery for Petrobras Supply Directorate kickbacks.
  • PMDB: Fernando Baiano, operator for PMDB’s International Directorate at Petrobras. Different directorate, different intermediary.
  • PT: João Santana, campaign consultant. Offshore payments disguised as consulting fees, channeled to PT campaign machinery.

Youssef could NOT deliver to PMDB — no relationship there. Baiano could NOT serve PP. Each intermediary was faction-specific: their value was in the delivery channel, not in bridging trust.

If intermediaries were trust brokers (Bernstein 1992), one well-connected broker would bridge across factions. Instead, each faction required its own operator.

The actual trust brokering was done by party leaders — Cunha (PMDB, Speaker of the House), Janene (PP) — who had relationships with BOTH firms and politicians and controlled access to state resources. They are the structural equivalent of Bernstein’s diamond dealer club president. The intermediaries (Youssef, Baiano, Santana) were logistics infrastructure serving those party-level brokers.

The real brokers

The structural parallel between judicial and congressional corruption:

Left: random case assignment means a litigant cannot build a bilateral relationship with the judge who will hear their case. Solution: hire a lawyer who appears before many judges regularly. The lawyer is the trust broker — repeated interaction on the many-judge side, market transaction on the client side.

Right: majority vote means a firm cannot bribe 257 MPs individually. Solution: pay a party leader who controls a voting bloc. The party leader is the trust broker — repeated interaction with MPs (campaign funds, committee appointments), market transaction with firms.

In both cases, institutional design creates the structural demand for brokering. The broker’s value is having repeated relationships on the many-to-one side.

Cunha (PMDB Speaker): “Eu não conheci ninguém que investiu tanto em política — pra ter poder de indicar cargos e tudo o mais” (Funaro testimony). He invested in relationships with dozens of MPs, then monetized that access.

Janene (PP): personally introduced Youssef to firms as “homem de confiança dele” — lending his reputation to guarantee the intermediary. Controlled both sides: installed Costa at Petrobras Supply and vouched for Youssef to firms.

The intermediaries (Youssef, Baiano) are the logistics layer BELOW the broker — they move cash, they don’t broker trust. The literature typically calls them “intermediaries” but their function is shielding, not brokering.

Of intermediaries labeled “consultant,” only 20% provide real services; 29% explicitly don’t; 51% unclear. The consulting label is the sham contract, not the function. - Real service (3%): actual consulting or legal work. - Remaining 46% have text too vague to classify by keyword.

Combined: most intermediaries move money (22%) or provide cover (14%), with no pre-existing official connection (75%). Only 15% provide access/influence. The typical intermediary is NOT a trust broker — they’re logistics infrastructure.

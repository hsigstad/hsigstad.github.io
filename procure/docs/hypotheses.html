Hypotheses

Hypotheses

Testable predictions connecting theory (docs/literature.md) to case evidence (docs/scheme_evidence.md). Organized in four thematic clusters aligned with paper/main.tex sections.

Cluster A: Scheme-Specific Bidding Signatures (H1–H4)

Maps to paper §7.2 (Bidding Signatures by Scheme Type).

H1. Fracionamento produces threshold bunching in contract values

Statement. Municipalities where judicial cases reveal fracionamento exhibit excess mass in the distribution of contract values just below dispensa and convite thresholds (R$8,000 / R$15,000 under Lei 8.666; R$50,000 / R$100,000 under Lei 14.133), relative to municipalities with no revealed corruption.

Theoretical motivation. Palguta and Pertold (2017) document bunching below discretionary thresholds in Czech procurement, with a threefold increase in awards to anonymous firms just below the cutoff. Caires, Peralta, and Mendes (2023) show Portuguese buyers split contracts to circumvent competitive requirements, motivated by favoritism. Carril (2021) finds that added scrutiny above thresholds distorts awards and discourages purchases, implying that corrupt actors face strong incentives to keep values below oversight triggers.

Case evidence. General Salgado (TJSP 0001731-89.2009.8.26.0204): two contracts of R$12,920 and R$5,225 to stay below the R$15,000 dispensa limit (scheme_evidence.md §1). Divinolândia (TJSP 1000487-42.2017.8.26.0588): successive verbal IT purchases below the R$8,000 dispensa threshold (§1). Jales (TJSP 1005280-19.2020.8.26.0297): insurance fragmented into small contracts enabling direct hiring (§1). TCU Referencial (Anexo 13) lists "contracting via dispensation with value just below the limit" as a specific red flag (§8).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variable: Excess mass in the contract-value distribution within narrow bins below each threshold (McCrary/Cattaneo density test).
  • Variation: Compare (i) municipalities with revealed fracionamento cases vs. matched controls, and (ii) contracts awarded by officials later convicted vs. contracts by the same municipality in non-convicted periods.
  • Specification: Density discontinuity at threshold $\bar{v}$; McCrary (2008) test or Cattaneo, Jansson, and Ma (2020) local polynomial density estimator applied to the contract-value distribution. For a difference specification, estimate $\hat{f}(v)$ separately for treated and control groups and test whether excess mass differs.
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, year, object category (CPV/object code).

Data requirements and limitations. Requires contract-level values from TCE-SP Audesp (available). Threshold values are known from legislation. Limitation: bunching may also reflect legitimate administrative convenience (passive waste), so a bunching test alone is necessary but not sufficient for a corruption interpretation — we sharpen identification by conditioning on revealed-corruption status.

H2. Shell companies produce anomalous bid patterns

Statement. Procurement processes involving empresas de fachada exhibit (a) lower bid dispersion (CV of bids), (b) co-participation clusters where the same set of firms repeatedly bid together, and (c) firm-level characteristics associated with shells (recent creation, shared addresses, laranja partner profiles).

Theoretical motivation. Harrington (2005) develops the logic behind screening for collusion using bid-level data, emphasizing that coordinated bidding compresses bid dispersion. Imhof (2018) operationalizes screens for cover bidding and related patterns, showing that the coefficient of variation of bids is a powerful discriminator. Conley and Decarolis (2016) propose statistical tests for coordinated entry and bidding, validated against Italian court-case data.

Case evidence. São José do Rio Preto (TJSP 1003519-62.2015.8.26.0576): entrepreneur created Mário Morales Navarro Construtora using a rural worker as laranja to simulate competition (§2). Adamantina (TJSP 0007153-26.2009.8.26.0081): FT Construções and HMI Engenharia submitted cover bids; grafotechnic analysis proved forged signatures (§2). Pinhalzinho (TJSP 0000703-26.2015.8.26.0447): Centro Diagnósticos and Clínica Negrini shared address and family partners (§2). CADE Guia (pp. 47–48) and MPSP Manual (p. 36) list identical bid documents, recent creation dates, and laranja profiles as detection indicators (§15).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: (i) CV of bid values within a process; (ii) co-bidding index — frequency with which firm pairs appear together across processes, relative to expected frequency under independent entry; (iii) shell-firm score based on CNPJ age, partner socio-economic profile, and shared-address indicator.
  • Variation: Processes linked to judicial cases involving empresas de fachada vs. observationally similar processes with no judicial link.
  • Specification: OLS/logit of outcome on treatment indicator (shell-company case), controlling for object type, estimated value, modality, municipality, and year. For co-bidding, adapt the Conley and Decarolis (2016) test to our setting: under the null of independent entry, the probability that firms $i$ and $j$ both participate in process $k$ equals the product of their individual participation probabilities.
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, year, object category, modality.

Data requirements and limitations. Bid-level data available from TCE-SP Audesp (bid values, participating CNPJs). Firm characteristics (creation date, address, partners) require linkage to Receita Federal CNPJ cadastral data (publicly available via QSA). Limitation: shell-firm indicators may flag some legitimate small firms; co-bidding tests require sufficient repeat participation.

H3. Cartel coordination produces bid rotation and winner concentration

Statement. In procurement segments where cartels have been judicially revealed, winning bids exhibit rotation patterns (sequential alternation of winners across processes for the same object), compressed winning margins, and abnormally high winner concentration (HHI) relative to segments with no revealed cartel.

Theoretical motivation. Harrington (2005) characterizes bid rotation and market division as core cartel strategies, producing predictable sequential patterns in winner identity. Kawai and Nakabayashi (2022) identify over 1,000 firms with non-competitive conduct using rebid data from Japanese procurement. OECD (2025) provides authoritative taxonomy of bid-rigging forms including rotation and compensation mechanisms.

Case evidence. Merenda Escolar (CADE PA 08012.007149/2009-39): rotation (rodízio) and geographic market division across 30 SP municipalities (scheme_evidence.md §3). Trens e Metrôs (CADE PA 08700.004617/2013-41): assignment by original equipment manufacturer, with "political agreements" accommodating all players (§3). TCU Codevasf (TC 005.920): Engefort won 74.6% (47 of 63) of collusive lots with discounts of only 0.01–1% (§§3, 15). Average winning discount fell from 24.5% to 5.32% as cartel consolidated (§15).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: (i) Rotation index — autocorrelation of winner identity across sequential processes for same object/municipality; (ii) winner HHI within object-municipality-year cells; (iii) winning margin (gap between winning and second-lowest bid).
  • Variation: Object-municipality cells linked to CADE or TJSP cartel cases vs. matched cells with no cartel link. For the Codevasf-style test: compare discount levels in periods before vs. after cartel formation.
  • Specification: Panel regression of outcome on cartel indicator, with pre-period placebo tests. For rotation: test whether the serial correlation of winner identity exceeds what a random assignment model would predict (permutation test).
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, object category, year.

Data requirements and limitations. Requires sequential process-level data with winner identity (available from Audesp). Cartel cases must be linked to specific object categories and municipalities. Limitation: bid rotation can also arise from capacity constraints in thin markets; we address this by conditioning on markets with sufficient participation and by testing for complementary indicators (compressed margins, cover bidding).

H4. Direcionamento produces low participation and single-bidder incidence

Statement. Procurement processes tainted by specification rigging (direcionamento) exhibit fewer bidders, higher single-bidder rates, and winning bids closer to the estimated value than comparable non-corrupt processes.

Theoretical motivation. Compte, Lambert-Mogiliansky, and Verdier (2005) model procurement with a bribable bureaucrat, showing that buyer-side corruption restricts competition by design. Decarolis et al. (2025) find that discretionary auctions are linked to corruption only when competition is restricted. Titl (2025) shows that a Czech reform prohibiting single-bid contracts reduced prices by 6.1%, confirming that single-bidder processes carry a corruption premium.

Case evidence. Vargem Grande do Sul (TJSP 1000316-79.2020.8.26.0653): restrictive clauses and brand-specific requirements limited competition to firms with shared partners (scheme_evidence.md §6). Pinhalzinho (TJSP 0000703-26.2015.8.26.0447): commission unduly disqualified a competitor to ensure pre-determined winner (§6). Itaí (TJSP 1006003-14.2018.8.26.0263): bidding commission adjusted process to ensure specific firm won, skipping mandatory price research (§6). CADE Guia (pp. 26–27) and TCU Referencial (Anexo 13) flag targeted specifications and unfair disqualifications (§15).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: (i) Number of bidders; (ii) indicator for single-bidder process; (iii) ratio of winning bid to estimated value.
  • Variation: Processes linked to judicial cases involving direcionamento vs. matched processes in the same municipality-year with no judicial link.
  • Specification: Poisson regression for bidder count; logit for single-bidder indicator; OLS for price ratio. Treatment is direcionamento-case link.
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, year, object category, modality.

Data requirements and limitations. Number of bidders and estimated value available from Audesp. Limitation: low participation can also reflect thin markets or unattractive contract terms; we control for object characteristics and use within-municipality variation to absorb time-invariant market thinness.

Cluster B: Post-Award Signatures (H5–H6)

Maps to paper §7.3 (Contract Execution Trajectories).

H5. Superfaturamento and propina produce larger and more frequent aditivos

Statement. Contracts linked to judicial cases involving overpricing (superfaturamento) or kickbacks (propina) exhibit (a) a higher probability of receiving any amendment, (b) more amendments per contract, and (c) larger cumulative amendment value as a share of original contract value, relative to non-corrupt contracts.

Theoretical motivation. Bajari, Houghton, and Tadelis (2014) estimate that adaptation costs in procurement amount to 7.5–14% of the winning bid even absent corruption, establishing a benchmark against which corrupt amendments can be measured. Campos et al. (2021) document how Odebrecht obtained favorable renegotiations through bribes. Schoenherr (2019) finds that contracts awarded to politically connected firms in Korea are executed worse, with more frequent cost increases.

Case evidence. Rodoanel Norte / Operação Pedra (ftlj_sp_denuncia_operacao_pedra.pdf): aditivos for "new" rock removal services already predicted in basic project, purely for overpricing (scheme_evidence.md §§5, 10). Metrô-SP (denuncia-metro-corrupcao.pdf): over 20 terms of extension to keep 1990 contracts alive, with kickbacks at 0.5% of monthly medições (§§5, 10). CGU Guia Section 3.C: jogo de planilha alters unit quantities in amendments to increase high-margin items (§5).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: (i) Indicator for any amendment; (ii) count of amendments; (iii) cumulative amendment value / original contract value.
  • Variation: Contracts linked to superfaturamento/propina cases vs. matched contracts by same municipality, year, and object type.
  • Specification: Logit for (i), Poisson for (ii), OLS/Tobit for (iii). Treatment: indicator for judicial link to superfaturamento or propina.
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, year, object category, modality.

Data requirements and limitations. Contract values and amendment values available from TCE-SP Audesp (ajustes and aditivos tables). Limitation: the 25% statutory cap on amendments (Art. 65 §1, Lei 8.666/1993) censors the distribution; contracts approaching the cap may reflect either corruption or genuine unforeseen circumstances. We address this by also examining amendment timing (early amendments more suspicious) and by conditioning on contract complexity proxies.

H6. Pre-award and post-award schemes have opposite bidding-amendment profiles

Statement. Contracts associated with pre-award schemes (specification rigging, bid steering) exhibit winning prices at or above estimated value with few subsequent amendments, whereas contracts associated with post-award schemes (jogo de planilha, kickbacks tied to medições) exhibit aggressive underbidding followed by large amendments. The two profiles are distinguishable in a price-amendment space.

Theoretical motivation. Iimi (2013) documents low-balling in rural road procurement, where firms undercut to win and then recover through cost and time overruns. Jung et al. (2019) show that bidding strategies increase markups for renegotiated items, consistent with bid-skewing as a deliberate strategy. CGU Guia Section 3.C defines jogo de planilha as altering budget composition to favor the contractor through amendments.

Case evidence. Pre-award profile: Vargem Grande do Sul (TJSP 1000316-79.2020.8.26.0653) — prices 19.5% above market, directed contract with minimal renegotiation (scheme_evidence.md §§5, 6). Jales (TJSP 1005280-19.2020.8.26.0297) — insurance 40% above market via direct contracting, no amendments (§§1, 6). Post-award profile: Rodoanel Norte aditivos for services already in project scope (§10). Metrô-SP: 20+ terms of extension with kickbacks anchored to monthly medições (§10).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: (i) Winning bid / estimated value ratio; (ii) cumulative amendment value / original value; (iii) joint distribution in price-amendment space.
  • Variation: LLM-classified scheme type (pre-award vs. post-award) from judicial narratives.
  • Specification: Multinomial classification of contracts into pre-award vs. post-award vs. clean, then compare mean profiles. Formally: interact scheme-type indicators with both price ratio and amendment ratio in a seemingly unrelated regression or bivariate model.
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, year, object category, modality.

Data requirements and limitations. Requires both bid-level data (winning bid, estimated value) and contract execution data (amendments) from Audesp. Scheme classification from LLM coding of judicial narratives. Limitation: the pre-award / post-award distinction is a simplification — some cases involve both phases. We handle mixed cases by allowing a "both" category and testing whether the profile is intermediate.

Cluster C: Networks and Institutional Conditions (H7–H9)

Maps to paper §8 (Network Analysis).

H7. Corrupt firm networks survive political turnover

Statement. Firms flagged in judicial cases during one mayoral administration continue winning procurement contracts at elevated rates under the subsequent administration, even after the implicated official has left office. Network persistence is higher than would be expected from firm-level incumbency advantage alone.

Theoretical motivation. Waxenecker and Prell (2024) find persistent collusive communities in Guatemalan procurement that weaken during crackdowns but re-emerge, characterizing corruption as a resilient network phenomenon. Fazekas, Tóth, and King (2016) map links between officeholders and winning firms across European contracts and find that high-centrality firms persistently win overpriced contracts.

Case evidence. Caconde (TJSP 0001827-77.2013.8.26.0103): municipal vehicles washed at businesses owned by Reginaldo Araújo Pinto across two different administrations (Antônio Carlos de Faria and Luciano Semensato) — network surviving administration change (scheme_evidence.md §7). Rodoanel (FTLJSP_denuncia_CARTEL_RODOSUL_SISVIARIO.pdf): when new state government ordered contract re-evaluations (Decreto Estadual 51.473), the G5 network adapted by renegotiating existing contracts for pre-selected lots in a new package (§7). Trens e Metrôs: cartel "renewed" coordination for each new maintenance cycle across administrations (§7).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variable: Firm's contract win rate and value share in period $t+1$ (next administration), conditional on being flagged in period $t$.
  • Variation: Compare flagged firms' persistence to that of non-flagged firms with similar pre-period win rates in the same municipality. Use mayoral turnover as natural variation in the political environment.
  • Specification: Difference-in-differences around administration changes: compare win rates of flagged vs. non-flagged firms before and after turnover. If networks are durable, the flagged-firm premium persists across the break.
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, firm, election cycle.

Data requirements and limitations. Requires firm-level panel of contract wins across electoral cycles (Audesp + TSE election data for administration boundaries). Limitation: firms may persist for legitimate reasons (local market power, specialization); we control for firm size, sector, and pre-period activity.

H8. Scheme complexity increases with municipality size

Statement. The distribution of scheme types varies systematically with municipality population: small municipalities are dominated by simple bilateral arrangements (fracionamento, direct dispensation abuse); medium municipalities exhibit cluster arrangements with 3–5 firms satisfying convite requirements; large municipalities feature shell-company networks, formal cartels, and multi-layer intermediation.

Theoretical motivation. Litschig and Zamboni (2024) show that municipalities with local judiciary have less resource diversion, suggesting institutional proximity shapes corruption form. Romero (2025) finds that more capable bureaucracies paradoxically increase the likelihood of connected firms winning through less competitive processes, implying that scheme sophistication co-evolves with administrative capacity.

Case evidence. Small: General Salgado (bilateral, R$12,920 + R$5,225 split) and Macaubal (mayor directing to niece's firm) (scheme_evidence.md §13). Medium: Adamantina (regional broker "Chiquinho do CDHU" using wife's firm and cover bidders) and Jales (brokerage intermediary managing systematic fracionamento) (§13). Large: São José do Rio Preto (shell network using rural workers as laranjas across multiple municipalities) and Ferraz de Vasconcelos (10-firm construction cartel) (§13).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variable: LLM-classified scheme type from judicial narratives, mapped to a complexity scale (bilateral = 1, cluster = 2, network = 3).
  • Variation: Municipality population (log) or administrative capacity proxies (number of civil servants, budget per capita).
  • Specification: Ordered logit of scheme complexity on municipality size, controlling for sector, year, and regional indicators. Alternative: multinomial logit with scheme-type categories as outcome.
  • Fixed effects: Year, mesoregion.

Data requirements and limitations. Scheme classification from LLM coding of TJSP sentenças. Population from IBGE census/estimates. Limitation: case selection by Ministério Público may vary with municipality size (larger municipalities attract more attention for complex schemes); we discuss this selection issue and, where possible, condition on audit intensity.

H9. Corrupt networks exhibit hub-and-spoke structure

Statement. In the bipartite firm-process network constructed from procurement data, processes linked to corruption cases are embedded in subnetworks with a hub-and-spoke topology: a small number of intermediary firms (hubs) connect many otherwise unrelated processes, producing a skewed degree distribution relative to non-corrupt subnetworks.

Theoretical motivation. Fazekas, Tóth, and King (2016) introduce corruption risk network indices based on firm centrality in procurement networks, finding that high-centrality firms win overpriced contracts. Harrington (2005) emphasizes the role of coordination mechanisms in sustaining collusion, which in network terms implies the existence of central nodes facilitating information flow.

Case evidence. Trens e Metrôs (trens_metros_voto.txt): consultancy Procint served as hub for meetings and market division models, with Alstom as "Leader" centralizing sensitive information (scheme_evidence.md §7). Rodoanel (FTLJSP_denuncia_CARTEL_RODOSUL_SISVIARIO.pdf): DERSA official Paulo Vieira de Souza acted as hub — "I manage the market" — dictating lot assignments to the G5 (§7). Merenda Escolar: Sindimerenda union as front for horizontal coordination meetings (§7).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: (i) Degree distribution of firms in the bipartite firm-process network; (ii) betweenness centrality of firms linked to corruption cases; (iii) ratio of max-degree to median-degree within corruption-linked subnetworks vs. clean subnetworks.
  • Variation: Compare network topology of corruption-linked subgraphs to random subgraphs of equal size drawn from the full procurement network.
  • Specification: Compute network statistics (degree distribution skewness, betweenness centrality, clustering coefficient) for corruption-linked and control subnetworks. Test differences using permutation inference.
  • Fixed effects: (Network analysis; not a regression.) Condition on municipality, year, and sector when constructing comparison subnetworks.

Data requirements and limitations. Requires firm-process participation data from Audesp (which firms bid on which processes). Limitation: hub structure may also arise from legitimate market leaders; we compare to randomly sampled subnetworks of equal size and density to assess whether the skewness is abnormal.

Cluster D: Detection and Deterrence (H10–H12)

Maps to paper §§9–10 (Robustness, Discussion).

H10. Higher audit intensity reduces bidding anomalies

Statement. Municipalities subject to higher TCE-SP audit intensity (more frequent inspections, more processes audited per year) exhibit lower prevalence of red-flag indicators — fewer single-bidder processes, less threshold bunching, lower winner concentration — controlling for municipality characteristics.

Theoretical motivation. Avis, Ferraz, and Finan (2018) find that audited Brazilian municipalities have ~8% less missing funds in subsequent audits, establishing a deterrence effect. Olken (2007) shows that increasing audit probability from 4% to 100% reduced missing expenditures by 8 percentage points in Indonesian road projects. Zamboni and Litschig (2018) find that higher perceived audit probability lowers procurement fraud, especially where legal consequences are stronger.

Case evidence. Bertioga (TJSP 1002040-48.2016.8.26.0075) and Itaí (TJSP 1006003-14.2018.8.26.0263): TCE-SP findings of irregularity triggered MP investigations, illustrating the audit-to-prosecution pipeline (scheme_evidence.md §9). TCE-SP reports flagging lack of basic projects and fragmented contracts were central evidence in several convictions (§14).

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: Composite red-flag index (single-bidder rate, threshold bunching, winner HHI, bid dispersion) at the municipality-year level.
  • Variation: TCE-SP audit intensity (number of specific audits per municipality-year) or quasi-random variation in audit assignment if available.
  • Specification: Municipality-year panel regression of red-flag index on audit intensity, controlling for municipality characteristics (population, budget, administrative capacity).
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, year.

Data requirements and limitations. Red-flag indicators computable from Audesp. Audit intensity requires TCE-SP activity data (number of audits per municipality-year). Limitation: audit assignment is likely endogenous (TCE-SP may target suspicious municipalities); we discuss this and, if possible, exploit plausibly exogenous variation in audit capacity (e.g., auditor staffing changes). This hypothesis serves primarily as a robustness check rather than a causal claim.

H11. Lei 14.133/2021 threshold increase shifts corruption away from fracionamento

Statement. After the adoption of Lei 14.133/2021 (with its higher dispensa thresholds of R$50,000 / R$100,000 vs. R$8,000 / R$15,000), bunching at the old Lei 8.666 thresholds disappears, and new bunching may emerge at the higher Lei 14.133 thresholds. The overall volume of fracionamento cases (as classified by LLM from judicial narratives) declines, but other scheme types (e.g., direcionamento within the expanded dispensa range) may increase.

Theoretical motivation. Palguta and Pertold (2017) show that threshold bunching responds to the specific values of legal cutoffs, implying that raising thresholds should eliminate bunching at old values. Carril (2021) demonstrates that scrutiny above thresholds distorts awards; if thresholds rise, the distortion zone shifts upward. The prediction of scheme substitution follows from rational adaptation: if fracionamento becomes less necessary (because the dispensa range now covers larger purchases), actors may substitute toward schemes that exploit the wider discretionary space.

Case evidence. Under the old regime: General Salgado split at R$15,000 (scheme_evidence.md §1); Divinolândia split at R$8,000 (§1). The new R$50,000 / R$100,000 thresholds (institutions.md §1.3) render these splits unnecessary. TCU Referencial (Anexo 13) explicitly flags threshold bunching as a red flag (§8), suggesting that enforcement bodies expect the pattern to migrate.

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variables: (i) Bunching statistic at old thresholds (R$8,000, R$15,000) vs. new thresholds (R$50,000, R$100,000); (ii) share of municipal procurement conducted via dispensa; (iii) LLM-classified scheme distribution (fracionamento share vs. other schemes).
  • Variation: Staggered adoption of Lei 14.133/2021 across municipalities (transition period allowed choice until December 2023).
  • Specification: Event study around municipality-level adoption date. Pre-adoption: bunching at old thresholds. Post-adoption: test for (a) disappearance of old bunching, (b) emergence of new bunching, (c) changes in dispensa share and scheme-type distribution.
  • Fixed effects: Municipality, year-month.

Data requirements and limitations. Contract-level data from Audesp with adoption date for each municipality (from municipal legislation or TCE-SP records). Limitation: the transition period (municipalities could choose old or new regime until December 2023) introduces selection — early adopters may differ systematically. We address this by using late-adopter municipalities as a control group in a staggered diff-in-diff design. Additionally, judicial cases identifying post-2021 schemes are scarce given the 7–8 year lag between acts and sentença; this hypothesis applies primarily to the procurement-data side.

H12. Composite red-flag index predicts independently revealed corruption

Statement. A composite index combining the red-flag indicators from H1–H5 (threshold bunching, bid dispersion, co-bidding clusters, winner concentration, amendment intensity) predicts which procurement processes are subsequently linked to judicial corruption cases, with predictive power above individual indicators.

Theoretical motivation. Decarolis and Giorgiantonio (2022) use ML tools to predict corruption from Italian tender characteristics, finding that combinations of red flags outperform individual indicators. Harrington and Imhof (2022) connect modern data methods to cartel detection, showing how composite screens are used by competition authorities and establishing that ML-based approaches can operationalize theoretically motivated markers.

Case evidence. TCU Codevasf (TC 005.920): identified "asphalt cartel" by combining discount pattern shifts (24.5% → 5.32%), winner concentration (Engefort winning 74.6% of lots), and low-discount wins (0.01–1%) (scheme_evidence.md §15). CADE Guia (Section II.4, pp. 47–49) lists indicators at four stages — bid submission, bidder declarations, bidder behavior, and outcomes — recommending combinations rather than individual flags (§15). Ferwerda and Deleanu (2013) find that combinations of red flags are the strongest predictors of corruption in EU procurement.

Empirical test.

  • Outcome variable: Binary indicator for whether the process is subsequently linked to a judicial corruption case (TJSP sentença).
  • Predictors: Threshold-bunching score, bid CV, co-bidding index, winner HHI, single-bidder indicator, amendment intensity, winning margin, reference-value proximity.
  • Specification: (i) Logistic regression with individual indicators and their composite index; (ii) random forest or gradient boosting for prediction. Evaluate with cross-validated AUC, comparing composite vs. individual indicators. (iii) SHAP values or permutation importance to identify which indicators drive predictions.
  • Fixed effects / controls: Municipality, year, object category, modality, estimated value (or included as features in ML model).

Data requirements and limitations. Requires all bidding and contract variables from Audesp plus the judicial-case linkage. Limitation: judicial cases are a selected sample of corruption (selection by MP, 7–8 year lag), so the model predicts detected and prosecuted corruption, not corruption per se. We discuss this explicitly and note that predictive power for detected cases is a lower bound on predictive power for all corruption if detection is positively correlated with indicator severity.

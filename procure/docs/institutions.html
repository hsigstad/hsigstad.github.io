Institutional Background

Single source of truth for all institutional facts used in the paper. Every factual claim must be sourced to a primary document before being written into paper/main.tex. Items marked NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE require verification against an authoritative original.

1. Brazilian Municipal Procurement

1.1 Lei 8.666/1993 — General Procurement Law

The 1993 statute governed virtually all public procurement in Brazil for nearly three decades and covers the bulk of our sample period.

  • Modalities (Art. 22): concorrência, tomada de preços, convite, concurso, leilão. Each defined by value thresholds and procedural requirements.
    • Convite (Art. 23, I, a / II, a): Engineering up to R$150,000; goods/services up to R$80,000. Minimum 3 invited firms (Art. 22 §3).
    • Tomada de preços (Art. 23, I, b / II, b): Engineering up to R$1,500,000; goods/services up to R$650,000. Requires prior cadastro.
    • Concorrência: Above tomada de preços thresholds; open to all.
    • Values updated via Decreto 9.412/2018.
    Source: Lei 8.666/1993 Art. 23; Decreto 9.412/2018.
  • Award criteria (Art. 45): menor preço (lowest price) is the default. Also: melhor técnica, técnica e preço, maior lance.
  • Publication requirements (Art. 21): Diário Oficial, newspapers; varies by modality and value. Creates paper trail exploited in our data.
  • Contract amendments (Art. 65): Permitted up to 25% of original value (50% for construction renovations). Art. 65 §1. Source: Lei 8.666/1993 Art. 65 §1.
  • Prohibited practices (Art. 90–98): Criminal provisions for fraud in bidding, including bid rigging (Art. 90), unlawful dispensation (Art. 89), and obstruction of competition (Art. 95). Source: Lei 8.666/1993, references/legislacao_licitacoes/lei_8666_1993.md

1.2 Lei 10.520/2002 — Pregão (Reverse Auction)

  • Introduced the pregão modality — an open descending-price auction.
  • Initially for goods and standard services; expanded in practice.
  • Pregão eletrônico (Decreto 10.024/2019): electronic version became mandatory for federal government, widely adopted by municipalities.
  • Pregão is the most common modality in municipal procurement in our sample period. NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: TCE-SP data on modality shares.

1.3 Lei 14.133/2021 — Nova Lei de Licitações

  • Replaces Lei 8.666/1993, Lei 10.520/2002, and the RDC (Lei 12.462/2011).
  • Transition period: municipalities could choose old or new regime until December 30, 2023 (Art. 191). After that date, Lei 14.133 is mandatory. NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: confirm Art. 191 transition deadline.
  • New modalities: diálogo competitivo added.
  • Dispensa thresholds (Art. 75): Art. 75, I: works/engineering up to R$100,000; Art. 75, II: goods/other services up to R$50,000. Values updated annually via Decree. Significantly higher than Lei 8.666 thresholds.
  • Strengthened integrity requirements: compliance programs, risk matrices.
  • Relevance for our paper: Most of our sample period is under the old regime. We note the transition but do not analyze its effects.

1.4 Dispensations and Inexigibilidade

  • Dispensa de licitação (Lei 8.666 Art. 24): Enumerated exceptions where competitive bidding is not required. Key cases:
    • Art. 24, I: works/engineering below R$15,000.
    • Art. 24, II: purchases/services below R$8,000.
    • Art. 24, IV: emergency situations.
    • Art. 24, V: no bidders appeared (deserto).
    Source: Lei 8.666/1993 Art. 24, I–II (original thresholds).
  • Anti-fracionamento rule (Art. 23 §5 of Lei 8.666/1993): Explicitly prohibits splitting works or services of the same nature that could be performed simultaneously or successively to circumvent modality thresholds. Lei 14.133/2021 Art. 75 §1 strengthens this by requiring agencies to sum all expenditures for "objects of the same nature" within a fiscal year.
  • Inexigibilidade (Art. 25): When competition is objectively infeasible (sole source, artistic services, specialized professionals).
  • Dispensations and inexigibilidade are frequently cited in improbidade cases as mechanisms for directing contracts without competition.

1.5 Municipal Procurement in São Paulo State

  • 645 municipalities in São Paulo state (644 in our TCE-SP data, excluding the capital for some analyses). NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: confirm exact count.
  • Municipalities are autonomous in procurement but report to TCE-SP.
  • Wide variation in administrative capacity: from São Paulo city (~12M population) to municipalities with fewer than 5,000 inhabitants.
  • NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: distribution of municipal procurement by value and modality from TCE-SP data.

2. Improbidade Administrativa and the Ministério Público

2.1 Lei 8.429/1992 — Lei de Improbidade Administrativa

The core statute for civil prosecution of corruption in public administration. Our TJSP case sample consists of ações de improbidade under this law.

  • Three categories of improbidade (Arts. 9–11):
    • Art. 9: Enriquecimento ilícito (illicit enrichment) — most severe.
    • Art. 10: Prejuízo ao erário (damage to the public treasury).
    • Art. 11: Violação de princípios (violation of administrative principles) — broadest category.
  • Sanctions (Art. 12): Loss of public function, suspension of political rights (up to 14 years for Art. 9), fines, prohibition from contracting with the government.
  • Prescription (Art. 23, pre-reform): 5 years from end of term for elected officials; 5 years from knowledge of the act for others. Post-2021 reform: 8-year absolute prescription from the act (Art. 23 of Lei 14.230/2021). STF ruled (RE 852475) that ressarcimento for intentional improbidade is imprescritível (no statute of limitations). Source: Lei 8.429/1992 Art. 23 (original); Lei 14.230/2021 Art. 23 (reformed); STF RE 852475.
  • Standing: Primarily the Ministério Público; also the affected public entity (Art. 17).

2.2 Lei 14.230/2021 — Reform of Improbidade

  • Major change: eliminated culposa (negligent) improbidade. After the reform, only dolosa (intentional) conduct is actionable. Source: Lei 14.230/2021 Art. 1 (amending Art. 1 §§1–3 of Lei 8.429).
  • Retroactivity: STF ruled (Tema 1199, decided August 2022) that the elimination of culposa applies retroactively to pending cases. NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: exact STF ruling and date.
  • Prescription reform: New 8-year absolute prescription from the act (Art. 23). Changed from the previous regime tied to end of office.
  • Impact on our sample: Cases filed before August 2022 under the old regime may have been affected. We document but do not analyze this.

2.3 The Ministério Público

  • Constitutional role (CF/88 Art. 127–130): Independent institution charged with defending the legal order, democratic regime, and social and individual interests.
  • Exclusive standing (post-2021 reform): Only the MP can bring improbidade actions (Lei 14.230/2021 removed standing from public entities). NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: confirm Art. 17 post-reform text.
  • Investigation process: Notícia de Fato (NF, must be processed within 120 days) → Inquérito Civil (IC, should conclude in 1 year, frequently extended) → Ação Civil Pública (ACP, filed before TJSP). Source: MPSP Manual de Investigação.
  • State-level MP (MP-SP): Brings improbidade cases before TJSP. Federal MP (MPF) handles cases involving federal funds or federal entities.
  • Selection of cases: MP exercises discretion in which cases to investigate and prosecute. This creates potential selection issues for our sample. NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: any MP-SP reports on case selection criteria or statistics on investigation-to-prosecution rates.

2.4 TJSP — São Paulo State Court of Justice

  • Largest state court in Brazil. Our universe of cases.
  • Jurisdiction: State-level improbidade cases involving municipal officials and procurement.
  • Case flow: MP files ação de improbidade → initial hearing → instruction → sentença (judgment at first instance) → possible appeal to TJ chambers.
  • Our data consists of sentenças (first-instance decisions) scraped from the TJSP electronic system.
  • Case duration: Typically 7–8 years from filing to first-instance sentença. CNJ study found average time to judgment in state courts of 1,586 days. Examples: Adamantina (0007153-26.2009.8.26.0081) took 8 years; São José do Rio Preto (1003519-62.2015.8.26.0576) took 8 years. Source: TJSP sentenças; CNJ study cited in MPSP Manual.
  • NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: TJSP annual report on improbidade case volume.

3. The Tribunal de Contas and Procurement Oversight

3.1 TCE-SP — Tribunal de Contas do Estado de São Paulo

  • Constitutional basis (CF/88 Arts. 70–75; São Paulo State Constitution): External control body that audits municipal public accounts.
  • Jurisdiction: All 644 non-capital municipalities in São Paulo state. (São Paulo capital has its own TCM-SP.) NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: confirm jurisdictional boundary and TCM-SP role.
  • Functions: Reviews annual accounts, audits specific acts (including procurement), issues opinions, can impose sanctions.

3.2 Audesp System

  • Automated Data Collection: Municipalities must upload detailed procurement and contract data to the Audesp (Auditoria Eletrônica de Órgãos Públicos) system.
  • Data reported: Licitações (bidding processes), ajustes (contracts), bid-level information, firm identifiers (CNPJ), contract values and amendments.
  • This is the source of our procurement data. We use the licitações and ajustes tables.
  • NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: TCE-SP resolution establishing Audesp reporting requirements. Data dictionary or schema documentation.

3.3 Oversight Mechanisms

  • Annual account reviews: TCE-SP issues parecer prévio on municipal accounts; municipal câmara votes on approval.
  • Specific audits: TCE-SP can audit individual procurement processes, especially those flagged by complaints or automated screening.
  • Sanctions: Multas, impugnação de atos, inabilitação of officials.
  • Relationship to improbidade: TCE-SP findings can trigger MP investigations but are separate proceedings. A TCE-SP finding of irregularity does not constitute a judicial finding of improbidade. NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: TCE-SP annual activity report on procurement audits and sanctions.

4. CADE and Cartel Enforcement

4.1 Lei 12.529/2011 — Competition Law

  • Established CADE (Conselho Administrativo de Defesa Econômica) as an autarquia with adjudicatory powers.
  • Cartel prohibition (Art. 36 §3, I): Agreements between competitors to fix prices, divide markets, or rig bids are administrative infractions.
  • Sanctions: Fines of 0.1% to 20% of gross revenue (Art. 37).
  • Leniency program (Art. 86–87): First confessor receives full or partial immunity. Key source of information on cartel mechanics. NEEDS PRIMARY SOURCE: exact leniency provisions and statistics on procurement-related cases.

4.2 Bid Rigging in Public Procurement

  • CADE published the Guia de Combate a Cartéis em Licitação (December 2019), consolidating 20+ years of institutional experience. Source: references/cade/guia_cartel_licitacao.pdf
  • CADE typology of collusive strategies (Part II, Section II.3):
    • II.3.1 Cover bidding (propostas fictícias ou de cobertura): Competitors submit deliberately high bids to make the designated winner's bid appear competitive. The most common form of collusion in procurement.
    • II.3.2 Bid suppression and withdrawal (supressão/retirada de propostas): Firms refrain from bidding or withdraw bids after submission to ensure the designated winner prevails.
    • II.3.3 Blocking in pregão presencial (bloqueio em pregão presencial): Specific to in-person reverse auctions. Cartel members submit bids within the 10% threshold of the lowest bid to block non-aligned firms from the verbal bidding phase. Does not apply to pregão eletrônico (footnote 36, p. 39).
    • II.3.4 Bid rotation (propostas rotativas ou rodízio): Firms take turns winning across different auctions or lots. Can be divided by equal number of lots, proportional to market share, or production capacity.
    • II.3.5 Market division (divisão de mercado): Geographic or client-based allocation among cartel members. Often combined with cover bidding or bid suppression.
    • II.3.6 Other strategies: Abuse of consórcios (Art. 33, Lei 8.666) and subcontracting to implement anticompetitive agreements and divide cartel profits.
  • Red flags for collusion (Section II.4, pp. 47–49): CADE lists indicators at four stages — (I) bid submission (few bids, unexplained withdrawals, bids with identical errors or formatting), (II) bidder declarations (references to "industry prices"), (III) bidder behavior (winners subcontracting losers, periodic wins of similar quantities), (IV) outcomes (rotation patterns, geographic concentration, disparate prices for similar objects).
  • Intersection of cartel and corruption (Part IV, pp. 52–58):
    • Cartel (agreement between competitors) and fraud (fake firms pretending to compete) are legally distinct but often coincide (p. 54).
    • Corruption (involving public officials) and cartel can coexist and reinforce each other. Officials may direct specifications or leak information to facilitate the cartel (p. 55).
    • The same conduct can be prosecuted in parallel:
      • Administrative infraction by CADE (Lei 12.529/2011 Art. 36).
      • Criminal offense under Lei 8.137/1990 Art. 4 and Lei 8.666/1993 Art. 90.
      • Improbidade administrativa by MP (Lei 8.429/1992).
      • Corporate liability under Lei Anticorrupção 12.846/2013 Art. 5.
    • Summary table on p. 58 maps sanctions, agreement types, and competent authorities across CADE, CGU, and MP/Police.
  • Procurement as share of GDP: OECD member countries ~15% of GDP; in non-member countries potentially higher. In Brazil, federal government alone conducted 100,000+ procurement processes totaling ~R$48 billion in 2018 (CADE guide, p. 12, citing Painel de Compras). Source: CADE guide p. 12; OECD (2009).

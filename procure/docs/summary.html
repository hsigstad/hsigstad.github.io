Research Summary

Summary

Research question

How is corruption in public procurement organized, and why does it take the specific forms it does? Economic theory predicts that corruption arrangements are shaped by observable conditions — market concentration, contract completeness, trust between parties, organizational structure, and monitoring technology. We ask whether these predictions hold empirically: do the corruption schemes revealed in judicial case files match the forms that theory would predict given the institutional and market environment?

Motivation

  • Public procurement is one of the largest channels through which governments transfer resources to the private sector
  • Theory offers clear predictions about how corruption should be organized: bilateral trust problems determine payment timing (Lambsdorff 2002), market structure determines whether firms collude or act alone (Stigler 1964), contract incompleteness determines where rents are extracted (Bajari and Tadelis 2001), and monitoring technology shapes which margins are exploited (Becker 1968)
  • But these predictions have never been systematically tested against detailed evidence on actual corruption mechanics — the theory exists in one literature, the case evidence in another, and the procurement data in a third
  • Anti-corruption policy is consequently generic: the same tools (audits, e-procurement, threshold rules) are applied regardless of the predicted scheme, leaving blind spots that corruption migrates to

Gap in the literature

The existing literature approaches procurement corruption from four directions that never meet:

  • Theory → predictions without evidence: Transaction-cost and mechanism design models (Lambsdorff 2002, Tirole 1986, Compte et al. 2005, Chassang and Ortner 2018) generate sharp predictions about corruption organization but are not tested against systematic case data. The predictions remain theoretical.
  • Data → inference without mechanisms: Detection papers (Harrington, Kawai & Nakabayashi, Imhof, Conley & Decarolis) start with bidding data and flag statistical anomalies but cannot identify the actual mechanism — was it bid rotation, specification tailoring, or a kickback with no bid manipulation at all?
  • Cases → binary outcome: Audit and enforcement papers (Ferraz & Finan, Avis et al., Olken, Zamboni & Litschig) use corruption revelations as a binary treatment. They study what happens when corruption is exposed but never classify which type of scheme was used or look for type-specific signatures in procurement data.
  • Typologies → no empirical link: The qualitative and practitioner literature (Søreide 2002, OECD guidelines, CADE guides, Konov & Gorbacheva 2023) provides rich taxonomies of how corruption works, but these are not systematically tested against actual procurement data. They remain descriptive.

No existing study connects theoretical predictions about corruption organization to systematic case evidence and measurable procurement outcomes.

The closest existing work:

  • Conley & Decarolis (2016) used court cases to validate detection screens, but did not classify scheme types or test structural predictions
  • Campos et al. (2021) provided a detailed case study of Odebrecht corruption mechanics, but N=1
  • Carbone (2024) links judicial evidence to auction participation, but narrower in scope

Approach

  • Assemble a comprehensive dataset linking the full universe of public contracts and bid histories to detailed judicial case files describing corruption mechanics
  • Use large language models to systematically classify scheme types from judicial narratives at scale
  • Link scheme-level classifications to procurement data to test whether observable conditions (market structure, contract type, relationship duration, monitoring intensity) predict scheme type as theory implies
  • Examine whether each scheme type produces a distinct procurement signature (bidding patterns, amendment trajectories, participation patterns)

Key contributions

  1. Bridge theory and evidence. First study to systematically test theoretical predictions about corruption organization against large-scale case evidence linked to procurement data. We show that scheme type is not random — it is structured by economic fundamentals.

  2. Theoretical framework. We synthesize predictions from five complementary frameworks:

    • Trust and commitment (Lambsdorff 2002, Dixit 2004): bilateral enforcement problems determine payment timing — pre-award bribes when the official doesn't trust the firm, post-award kickbacks anchored to contract milestones when the firm doesn't trust the official
    • Market structure (Stigler 1964): concentrated markets sustain cartels with inflated bids; competitive markets force unilateral strategies — underbidding followed by post-award extraction through amendments
    • Contract incompleteness (Bajari and Tadelis 2001): complex contracts create room for post-award manipulation (jogo de planilha, aditivos); complete contracts force pre-award rent extraction (overpricing, specification rigging)
    • Hierarchical collusion (Tirole 1986): the weakest monitoring link in the bureaucratic chain determines which scheme is feasible — complicit fiscal enables post-award fraud, complicit comissão enables bid steering
    • Detection and substitution (Becker 1968, Chassang and Ortner 2018): improvements in monitoring at one margin push corruption to less monitored margins — the waterbed effect

  3. Empirical findings. Evidence consistent with the theoretical predictions:

    • Judicial case files reveal operational mechanics that match predicted forms: cartels in concentrated markets, bilateral steering in competitive markets, post-award extraction in complex contracts
    • Each scheme type has a distinct bidding signature and ex post trajectory (pre-award schemes show no genuine competition and prices above reference values; post-award schemes show underbidding followed by large amendments)
    • Corruption is embedded in durable network structures whose topology (hub-and-spoke vs. peer-to-peer) reflects the coordination technology predicted by theory

  4. Policy implications. Anti-corruption tools must be matched to the predicted scheme:

    • Bid-level screening catches cartels but misses post-award extraction
    • Amendment monitoring catches jogo de planilha but misses specification rigging
    • Threshold rules prevent fracionamento but create opportunities for direcionamento within the expanded dispensa range
    • One-size-fits-all approaches leave blind spots, and corruption migrates to unmonitored margins
    • Effective policy requires diagnosing which scheme the institutional environment predicts and targeting monitoring accordingly

What makes this feasible

The project exploits a rare combination of data:

  • Full-text judicial decisions (12,000+ improbidade administrativa cases from TJSP) that describe in detail how corruption was organized — the specific scheme, the actors, the mechanics
  • Bid-level municipal procurement data (TCE-SP licitações + ajustes) for 644 municipalities with firm identifiers, bid values, and contract outcomes
  • A linking chain from court text → procurement process numbers → contract and bid records
  • LLMs that can classify scheme types from judicial narratives at scale, replacing what would otherwise require thousands of hours of expert legal coding

Most countries lack all three ingredients. Brazil's combination of detailed judicial narratives, granular procurement data, and linkable identifiers creates a unique empirical opportunity. The judicial narratives provide the mechanism; the procurement data provide the signature; the theory provides the prediction connecting the two.

Setting

  • Brazil, São Paulo state, municipal level
  • Data sources: TJSP judicial case files (sentenças), TCE-SP procurement records (licitações + ajustes/contracts), CGU audit reports

Possible directions

Confirmed: court cases are about municipal procurement

The court cases in example_procurement_cases.csv are overwhelmingly about municipal procurement fraud:

  • assunto is almost always "Improbidade Administrativa" or "Dano ao Erário"
  • comarcas span small municipalities across SP (Pontal, Cunha, Igarapava, Tatui, Santos, etc.)
  • cases describe prefeitos directing convites, tomadas de preços, and dispensas at municipal level
  • the procurement numbers (e.g., "convite nº 26/2010 do Município de Pontal") are municipal procedure numbers

The BEC data covers state-level procurement (Secretaria da Educação, etc.) — a different universe.

Confirmed: TCE-SP has bid-level municipal data

The TCE-SP licitação files contain genuine bid-level data:

  • unit of observation: bidder × item × procurement event
  • fields: firm CNPJ, firm name, bid value, outcome (winner/disqualified/no-show)
  • procurement modalities: 97% Pregão Eletrônico, plus Pregão Presencial, Dispensa, Concorrência, Convite, Tomada de Preços, Inexigibilidade
  • scale: ~7.4M rows per month; 500+ municipalities per month
  • coverage: 2018–2022 (saude), Nov–Dec 2024 (procure)

The ajustes (contract) files link to licitação files and add:

  • Numero do processo licitatório + Ano do processo licitatório — the internal municipal process number, which is what court cases cite
  • Codigo da Licitacao — TCE-SP internal code linking to bid-level data
  • contract details: CNPJ, firm name, contract number/dates/values, empenho amounts

Primary linking strategy: court cases → ajustes → licitações

Court case text  →  extract (municipality, modality, processo nº, year)
                           ↓
Ajustes data     →  match on (municipality + processo nº + year) → Codigo da Licitacao
                           ↓
Licitação data   →  join on Codigo da Licitacao → full bid-level detail
  • step 1: extract structured identifiers from court case text (regex or LLM)
  • step 2: join to ajustes on (Municipio + Numero do processo licitatório + Ano do processo licitatório)
  • step 3: use Codigo da Licitacao to get all bids from the licitação files

Complementary strategies

Firm-based linking (no process number needed)

  • extract firm CNPJ from court case text (~3% have explicit CNPJ)
  • extract firm names from court case text (most cases mention firms)
  • match to CNPJ do participante candidato / Nome do participante candidato in licitação data
  • or match to CNPJ da empresa contratada / Nome da empresa contratada in ajustes
  • this finds all procurement activity by firms named in court cases, not just the specific procurement at issue

Municipality-level linking

  • even without matching specific procurements, we can identify which municipalities have corruption cases
  • then study the universe of procurement in those municipalities vs. clean municipalities
  • this is a weaker but higher-coverage strategy

BEC state-level data: secondary role

  • BEC covers state procurement only
  • may be useful for the subset of cases involving state-level purchases
  • the jud_xxx flag (18,333 OCs, 2.4%) could be a validation set
  • keep as supplementary, not primary

What the data exploration revealed

Court case text (3,691 cases)

  • 99.5% mention "licitação" somewhere
  • 70.9% mention "dispensa" (dispensation from competitive bidding)
  • 37.5% mention "convite", 33% "concorrência", 33% "inexigibilidade", 14.6% "pregão", 12.4% "tomada de preços"
  • ~25% have a procurement modality + number in extractable "nº XX/YYYY" format
  • 89% have extractable municipality names
  • 87% have R$ monetary values
  • only 2.6% have CNPJ patterns
  • 26% mention fracionamento, 27% superfaturamento, 24% direcionamento
  • many cases describe irregularities where no formal procurement number exists (contratação sem licitação)

BEC edital files (8 sample files)

  • all are Convite modality, from Secretaria da Educação
  • only identifier is OC number (= filename), no separate "procedimento licitatório" number
  • the edital crosswalk does not work as originally hypothesized (at least for Convite)

BEC procurement data (state-level)

  • consolidated: ~4M rows, 764,538 unique OCs
  • modalities: 49% Convite, 35% Pregão Eletrônico, 15% Dispensa
  • pre-existing jud_xxx flag on 18,333 OCs (2.4%)

TCE-SP municipal licitação data (bid-level)

  • ~7.4M rows per month
  • unit: bidder × item × procurement event
  • outcome field: Classificado - Vencedor (6%), Classificado (11%), Desclassificado (18%), Não Compareceu (61%)
  • 500+ municipalities, 5,000+ licitações per month
  • Numero do edital field present but inconsistently formatted

TCE-SP ajustes data (contract-level)

  • ~4M rows total across 2018–2022
  • has Numero do processo licitatório + Ano do processo licitatório — the key linking field
  • links to licitação via Codigo da Licitacao
  • has contracted firm CNPJ and name, contract values, dates
  • 244,465 unique (municipality, proc_num, proc_year) keys across 644 municipalities
  • processo years in ajustes are concentrated in 2016–2022 (sparse before 2016)

Match test results: court cases → ajustes

See docs/briefs/linking.md for cohesive narrative. Methods in docs/methods.md.

Key finding: the linking chain works well mechanically — match rates of 50–88% within the TCE-SP time window (2017+). The main bottleneck is time period: most court cases reference pre-2016 procurement while ajustes covers 2016–2022.

Trust, commitment, and scheme selection

Corrupt deals cannot be enforced in court (Lambsdorff 2002). The form of the arrangement therefore depends on who bears performance risk — who moves first and can be cheated. This generates predictions about which scheme type arises under which conditions.

The bilateral trust problem

Consider a firm (F) and a politician/official (P) contemplating a corrupt deal. Two actions must happen: P steers a contract toward F, and F pays P a bribe. Neither action is legally enforceable. The sequencing of these actions depends on who trusts whom.

Case 1: F does not trust P (but P trusts F). F will not pay upfront — it fears P will pocket the bribe and not deliver. So the bribe is anchored to observable post-award events that F controls. F pays only as P delivers: approving medições, signing aditivos, certifying completion. This produces post-award kickback schemes where payment flows track contract execution.

Case evidence: Metrô-SP kickbacks at 0.5% of monthly medições (scheme_evidence.md §4). The firm controls payment timing — it pays only after the official approves the measurement. Rodoanel bribes embedded in "Amor vs. Briga" pricing, paid out during execution (§4).

Case 2: P does not trust F (but F trusts P). P will not steer without assurance. Two solutions: (a) F pays upfront, before the award; or (b) P extracts rents through the procurement design itself, so that the "bribe" is embedded in the act of steering — no separate payment needed. This produces pre-award schemes: specification rigging (direcionamento), fracionamento to direct to a specific firm, or upfront cash payments.

Case evidence: Paraibuna bribe of R$9,000 deposited into Mayor's account before homologation (§4). Vargem Grande do Sul prices set 19.5% above market via directed contract — P's rent is the political favor, not a cash kickback (§§5, 6). São José do Rio Preto "Grupo Faria" giving luxury cars and trips before the official assumes office (§12).

Case 3: Neither trusts the other. The deal requires a trust technology:

  • Repeated interaction builds reciprocity (Lambsdorff and Frank 2011). Maps to the durable networks in §7: Merenda cartel (20 years), Trens/Metrôs (15 years), Metrô-SP extensions (14 years of aditivos).
  • Intermediaries who can punish defection (Drugov, Hamman, and Serra 2014). Maps to Procint in trens/metrôs, Paulo Vieira de Souza as "market administrator" in Rodoanel (§7), brokers like Ademar Bocalon in Jales (§12).
  • Third-party enforcement by organized crime. Maps to PCC in Operação Munditia, military police coercion in Operação Prenunciado (§§2, 3).

Case 4: Both trust each other (long relationship). Schemes become more complex and efficient: jogo de planilha, sophisticated cartel rotation with compensation mechanisms, multi-layer intermediation. The parties can coordinate across multiple transactions because the shadow of future interaction disciplines both sides (Dixit 2004).

Testable predictions

This framework generates predictions that refine H6:

  1. New firm-politician pairs → simpler, more self-enforcing arrangements (pre-award steering, upfront payment, or post-award payment anchored to verifiable milestones). Predicted signature: direcionamento with few amendments, or single large kickback.

  2. Repeat pairs → more complex post-award extraction (jogo de planilha, serial aditivos, subcontracting to cartel losers). Predicted signature: aggressive underbidding followed by large amendments.

  3. Intermediary-mediated deals → schemes involving third parties (brokers, consultants, organized crime). Predicted signature: hub-and-spoke network topology (H9), cross-municipality coordination.

  4. Municipality size as proxy for trust infrastructure → small municipalities (bilateral, personal ties) should exhibit pre-award schemes; larger municipalities (repeat players, intermediaries) should exhibit post-award extraction. This refines H8 with a mechanism.

Empirical strategy

The key variation is relationship duration between firm and official:

  • Measure number of prior contracts between the same CNPJ and municipality (or same CNPJ and mayoral administration) before the corrupt event.
  • Interact relationship duration with scheme type (LLM-classified) to test whether longer relationships predict more complex, post-award schemes.
  • Use first-time firm-municipality pairs as a benchmark for the "no trust" prediction.

Open questions

  • Can we observe intermediaries in the procurement data (e.g., subcontracting patterns, co-bidding with hub firms)?
  • Does the trust framework predict anything about bribe size or corruption efficiency (rent extraction per transaction)?
  • How does the Lei 14.230/2021 dolo requirement interact? If only intentional acts are punishable, does this change the trust calculus (lower cost of defection for the politician)?

Hierarchical collusion and organizational position

Tirole (1986) models a three-tier hierarchy (principal–supervisor–agent) where the supervisor and agent can collude against the principal. The key insight: the position of the colluding party in the hierarchy determines what kind of manipulation is feasible, because each position controls different information and decision rights.

Application to procurement

The procurement chain has multiple layers, each controlling a different gate:

Position Controls Enables which scheme
Prefeito / Secretário Budget allocation, emergency declarations, dispensa authorization Dispensa abuse, fabricated emergencies, fracionamento (splitting across budget lines)
Assessor jurídico Legal opinions on modality choice and dispensation legality Legal cover for irregular dispensas; generic favorable opinions (Itaí §6, Dracena §12)
Comissão de licitação Qualification decisions, bid evaluation, disqualification Undue disqualification of competitors (Pinhalzinho §6), accepting irregular docs from favored firms (Adamantina §6)
Fiscal de contrato Certifying delivery, approving medições, signing completion reports Post-award phantom delivery (São Pedro §10), accepting inferior quality (Adamantina §10)
Technical staff Drafting specifications, estimating reference prices Specification rigging / direcionamento (Rodoanel §6), inflated reference prices (§5)

Predictions

  1. The weakest monitoring link determines the corruption form. If the fiscal de contrato is the weak point, corruption takes a post-award form (phantom delivery, measurement fraud). If the comissão is the weak point, corruption is pre-award (bid steering, disqualification). This is testable: municipalities where the fiscal is a political appointee (vs. career civil servant) should show more post-award anomalies.

  2. Multi-layer collusion enables more complex schemes. When both the comissão and the fiscal are complicit, you get the full cycle: directed award followed by inflated execution (Bertioga: fracionamento + directed convite + inferior quality). The number of complicit layers predicts scheme complexity — refining H8 with an organizational mechanism rather than just municipality size.

  3. Redundant monitors reduce collusion (Kofman and Lawarree 1993). TCE-SP audits serve as an external monitor that is harder to co-opt than internal supervisors. This gives a mechanism for H10 (audit intensity reduces anomalies): external audits break the supervisor-agent coalition.

Empirical hook

The TCE-SP Audesp data may allow us to identify the fiscal de contrato or comissão composition for some municipalities. Cross-referencing with personnel data (if available) could test whether political appointees in these roles correlate with specific scheme types.

Market structure and the cartel vs. bilateral corruption choice

Stigler (1964) identifies the structural conditions that sustain collusion: few sellers, homogeneous products, observable transactions, frequent interaction. These same conditions determine whether procurement corruption takes the form of horizontal cartel coordination among firms or vertical bilateral corruption between a single firm and an official.

Predictions from IO theory

Concentrated markets with few repeated specialized firms → cartels. When the same 5–10 firms compete repeatedly for similar objects (train maintenance, school meals, asphalt paving), they can sustain rotation and market division agreements. Detection of cheating is easy because bids are observable. Entry barriers are high (specialized expertise, equipment).

Case evidence: Trens/Metrôs cartel (19 firms, specialized rail maintenance), Merenda Escolar (food service companies across 30 municipalities), TCU Codevasf "asphalt cartel" (paving contractors).

Fragmented markets with many substitutable firms → bilateral corruption or specification rigging. When many firms could supply the good (office supplies, basic construction materials, vehicle insurance), sustaining a cartel is difficult — too many players, low barriers to entry, hard to monitor cheating. Instead, the corrupt official uses specification rigging or fracionamento to select a specific firm, bypassing competition altogether.

Case evidence: Jales (insurance directed to specific broker), Divinolândia (IT equipment via verbal contracts), General Salgado (basic infrastructure split to avoid competition).

Intermediate markets → hybrid schemes. Shell companies bridge the gap: in markets with moderate concentration, the favored firm creates phantom competitors to simulate the minimum participation required by law (3 firms for convite). This is neither pure cartel (firms aren't genuinely independent) nor pure bilateral (there is a facade of competition).

Case evidence: Adamantina (3–5 related firms filling convite quota), São José do Rio Preto (shell company using laranja to simulate competition).

Market thickness and the bidding-amendment profile

The number of actors determines not just whether corruption occurs but where in the procurement cycle the rent is extracted:

Thin market (few specialized firms) → pre-award rent extraction. Coordination is feasible (Stigler 1964). Cartel inflates bids; the corrupt surplus is embedded in the price at the bidding stage. Amendments are unnecessary for rent extraction (though they may occur for other reasons). Evidence: Merenda Escolar (FIPE estimated 72% overpricing vs. self-managed meals), Codevasf asphalt cartel (winning discounts collapsed from 24.5% to 5.32%), Trens/Metrôs (16–25% higher prices in cartel rounds).

Thick market (many potential bidders) → post-award rent extraction. Coordination across many firms is infeasible. A single corrupt firm must win against genuine competition, so it underbids — then recovers through post-award manipulation: jogo de planilha (shifting quantities to high-markup items), aditivos for "unforeseen" conditions, measurement fraud. This is a unilateral strategy that requires no coordination with competitors, only complicity from the fiscal de contrato or the contracting authority. CGU Guia §3 explicitly describes jogo de planilha as independent of bidding-phase coordination. Iimi (2013) and Jung et al. (2019) document the underbidding + renegotiation pattern empirically. Campos et al. (2021) find 70.8% cost overruns on Odebrecht's bribed projects vs. 5.6% on clean ones — Odebrecht operated in markets where it could not control all competitors and extracted rents through favorable renegotiations.

Concentrated markets can do both. The G5 in Rodoanel coordinated pre-award (cartel rotation, cover bidding) AND extracted post-award (aditivos for rock removal already in the project). The theory predicts thick markets are limited to post-award extraction; it does not predict thin markets can't also use post-award mechanisms. The asymmetry is one-directional.

Testable prediction. Interact market HHI (by object category × municipality) with the bidding-amendment profile from H6. In high-HHI (concentrated) corrupt markets: high winning bids, few amendments. In low-HHI (competitive) corrupt markets: low winning bids, large amendments. The two profiles should separate in price-amendment space, with market concentration as the moderator.

Key distinction from trust framework

The trust framework (above) predicts when the bribe is paid (pre- vs. post-award). The market structure framework predicts how competition is suppressed (cartel rotation vs. specification rigging vs. shell companies). These are orthogonal dimensions: a cartel can use either pre-award bribes (paying officials to leak edital details) or post-award extraction (subcontracting losers). The two frameworks are complementary.

Empirical hook

Market concentration (HHI of bidders by object category × municipality) is directly computable from Audesp. Prediction: high-HHI markets linked to corruption show cartel signatures (rotation, cover bidding); low-HHI markets linked to corruption show direcionamento signatures (single bidder, restrictive specs). This refines H3 and H4 by conditioning on market structure.

Contract incompleteness and sectoral vulnerability

Bajari and Tadelis (2001) model the buyer's choice between fixed-price and cost-plus contracts as a trade-off between incentive power and adaptation costs. When project design is complex and changes likely, the contract is necessarily incomplete — and incompleteness creates room for post-award manipulation.

The logic

Contract incompleteness varies systematically by procurement sector:

High incompleteness (construction, IT, consulting):

  • Many contingencies cannot be specified ex ante (soil conditions, design changes, scope evolution)
  • Amendments (aditivos) are expected and legally permitted (Art. 65, Lei 8.666: 25% for services, 50% for renovations)
  • Post-award manipulation is feasible: jogo de planilha (shifting quantities between line items), aditivos for "unforeseen" conditions already predicted in the basic project, measurement fraud
  • The corrupt surplus is extracted during execution, not at the bidding stage

Low incompleteness (commodities: fuel, food, uniforms, medicine):

  • Specifications are standardized; quantities and quality are verifiable
  • Little room for post-award manipulation — what you ordered is what you get
  • Corruption must happen pre-award: overpricing (inflated reference prices), specification rigging (brand requirements), fracionamento (splitting to avoid competition)
  • The corrupt surplus is embedded in the price, not extracted through amendments

Predictions

  1. Construction and IT contracts linked to corruption should show the post-award profile (H6): competitive or low initial bids followed by large amendments. Firms underbid to win, then recover through aditivos.

  2. Commodity purchases (food, fuel, medicine, office supplies) linked to corruption should show the pre-award profile: prices at or above reference value, few amendments, but low competition (single bidder or directed award).

  3. The amendment rate is higher in corrupt construction contracts than in corrupt commodity contracts, even controlling for baseline amendment rates by sector.

This transforms the descriptive scheme-sector associations in scheme_evidence.md §11 into theoretically grounded predictions.

Interaction with trust framework

Contract incompleteness also affects the bilateral trust problem. In complex contracts, the firm has more information about true costs than the official (information asymmetry favoring the firm). This makes it harder for the official to verify whether the firm is delivering value. The official's response: either demand upfront payment (pre-award bribe) or anchor kickbacks to easily observable milestones (monthly medições). Laffont and Tirole (1993) formalize how information asymmetry shapes optimal contract design — the corrupt version is that the same asymmetry shapes the "optimal" corrupt arrangement.

Empirical hook

Object category codes in Audesp allow classification of contracts by incompleteness level (construction/IT vs. commodities). Interact sector incompleteness with corruption indicator to test whether the bidding-amendment profile differs by sector as predicted.

Detection technology and scheme substitution

Becker (1968) frames crime as a rational choice: the offender weighs expected benefits against expected costs (probability of detection × sanction). Applied to corruption scheme choice: actors select the scheme with the highest risk-adjusted return, and any change in monitoring at one margin pushes corruption to less monitored margins.

The waterbed effect in procurement

Olken (2007) provides direct evidence: when Indonesian communities monitored road project prices more closely, officials shifted corruption to quantities (thinner roads) — substitution within a project. Yang (2008) finds analogous substitution in customs: tightening enforcement against price undervaluation caused smugglers to shift to tariff misclassification.

In procurement, monitoring technology has changed dramatically:

Monitoring change What it monitors Predicted displacement
Pregão eletrônico (e-auction) Bid values, participation, real-time observation Away from cover bidding → toward dispensas, post-award manipulation, or specification rigging that prevents entry
TCE-SP Audesp (bid-level data) Bid patterns, winner concentration Away from bid-rotation cartels → toward post-award schemes (aditivos, measurement fraud) or corruption in dispensas not captured in bid data
Lei 14.133/2021 higher thresholds Threshold bunching at old values disappears Away from fracionamento at old thresholds → toward direcionamento within expanded dispensa range (already H11)
CGU random audits General procurement irregularities Away from easily auditable schemes (missing documents) → toward schemes requiring technical expertise to detect (jogo de planilha, inflated reference prices)

Predictions

  1. After pregão eletrônico adoption, municipalities should show fewer cover-bidding anomalies but more post-award anomalies (aditivos) and more dispensas (shifting to the unmonitored margin). This is testable with staggered e-procurement adoption across municipalities.

  2. Municipalities with higher TCE-SP audit intensity should show less of the "easy" corruption (missing documents, obvious fracionamento) but not necessarily less of the "hard" corruption (jogo de planilha, inflated reference prices). The composite red-flag index (H12) should capture different indicators in high- vs. low-audit municipalities.

  3. Over time, as monitoring technology improves, the composition of corruption should shift toward more technically sophisticated schemes, even if the level of corruption decreases. This is a dynamic prediction that complements H10 (static audit intensity effect).

Connection to Ortner and Chassang (2018)

Chassang and Ortner show that randomizing the monitor's incentives can make collusion harder by introducing asymmetric information between corrupt parties. Applied to procurement: unpredictable audit focus (randomizing which aspects of procurement are scrutinized) is more effective than predictable rules, because predictable rules allow actors to concentrate corruption on unmonitored dimensions. This has implications for TCE-SP audit design.

Empirical hook

The staggered adoption of pregão eletrônico and the transition from Lei 8.666 to Lei 14.133 provide quasi-experimental variation. Compare the composition of red-flag indicators (not just the level) before and after each reform. If substitution occurs, the aggregate red-flag index may show little change even as individual indicators shift.

Entry deterrence as corruption tool

Clark, Coviello, Gauthier, and Shneyerov (2018) study Montreal's asphalt cartel and find that anti-collusion enforcement increased the number of bidders by over 60% — implying the cartel had been actively deterring entry. Auriol (2006) models how a corrupt procurement officer captures rents by restricting competition. The corrupt network isn't just exploiting low competition — it actively creates and maintains barriers to entry.

Mechanisms of entry deterrence

From the case evidence:

Low-cost deterrence (thin markets, few potential entrants):

  • Restrictive specifications (Vargem Grande do Sul: brand-specific requirements, §6)
  • Excessively high qualification requirements (capital social, technical experience disproportionate to contract value, §6)
  • Undue disqualification of entrants (Pinhalzinho: competitor disqualified on manufactured technicalities, §6)

→ Produces "no competition" schemes: single bidder, directed award.

High-cost deterrence (thick markets, many potential entrants):

  • Physical intimidation (Operação Prenunciado: police officers at bidding sessions threatening competitors, §3)
  • Organized crime enforcement (Operação Munditia: PCC-linked network, §2)
  • Reputation for retaliation ("chumbo cruzado" in trens/metrôs: firms threatened with legal challenges on other projects, §3)

→ Produces "fake competition" schemes: only network members participate, submitting cover bids. Outsiders are deterred, not excluded by rules.

Intermediate: shell companies as synthetic entry barriers. The network fills the minimum bidder requirement (3 for convite) with its own entities, making the market appear competitive while effectively being closed.

Predictions

  1. Entry deterrence cost determines the form of competition suppression. In thin markets (few potential entrants), low-cost specification rigging suffices — prediction: single-bidder processes, high reference-value proximity. In thick markets (many potential entrants), deterrence requires active effort — prediction: cover bidding, shell companies, or intimidation, but with more bidders (all fake).

  2. After cartel disruption, entry increases sharply (Clark et al. 2018 find 60%+ increase). In our data: municipalities where GAECO operations dismantled cartels should show a jump in the number of unique bidders for the same object category.

  3. The number of bidders is not a sufficient corruption indicator. In fake-competition schemes, the number of bidders may look normal while the composition is corrupt (all related firms). This reinforces H2 (co-bidding clusters) over H4 (low participation) as the more robust indicator for cartel-type corruption.

Empirical hook

Compare number of unique bidders and bidder turnover in object-municipality cells before and after GAECO operations or CADE decisions. The pre-disruption period should show stable, low turnover (same firms repeatedly); the post-disruption period should show entry by new CNPJs. This is a direct test of whether the cartel was deterring entry.

    Theory

    Why family connections can bias judicial decisions

    <!-- slug: bias-mechanisms -->

    The adversarial legal system assumes an impartial adjudicator. When a judge and a lawyer are family members, two forces can distort this:

    1. Direct reciprocity. The judge favors the connected lawyer's client, either out of familial obligation or in expectation of material return (the connected lawyer commands higher fees and may share rents). This is corruption in the narrow sense — a collusive arrangement within a public hierarchy (Tirole 1986; Kofman and Lawarree 1993).

    2. Implicit bias. Even absent deliberate favoritism, familiarity and trust between judge and lawyer can shift discretionary margins — scheduling, evidentiary rulings, settlement pressure — in ways that benefit the connected side. The judge need not be consciously corrupt; the bias operates through soft channels of affinity and credibility (Verhagen and Yam 2021; Metcalfe 2016).

    Both forces predict that connections increase the connected side's probability of winning and the value awarded. They differ in whether the judge is a strategic actor or a boundedly rational one, but are observationally similar in our setting. Our design — random assignment of cases to judges — produces a "forensic" causal signature (Zitzewitz 2012) that identifies the combined effect without requiring us to decompose the mechanism.

    Why the effect depends on lawyer incentives

    <!-- slug: lawyer-incentives -->

    The plaintiff-defendant asymmetry in our results — strong effects for plaintiff-side connections, near-zero for defendant-side — maps onto the incentive structure of legal representation:

    • Plaintiff lawyers work on contingency fees (typically 20–30% of the value awarded). A favorable ruling directly increases the lawyer's payoff. This creates joint surplus between judge and lawyer: the judge's favor translates into rents that the connected lawyer captures. The lawyer has a strong incentive to seek out and activate the connection, and the judge's favor is worth more.

    • Defendant lawyers work on fixed wages, independent of case outcome. Even if a connected judge were willing to favor the defendant side, the lawyer captures none of the surplus. There is no rent to share, and therefore weaker incentives for either side to activate the connection.

    This is a standard moral hazard argument: connections only distort outcomes when the agent (lawyer) has incentive contracts that make the connection valuable. Fixed-wage contracts insulate the relationship from generating bias, not because the connection is weaker, but because neither party can monetize it. The general principle — that strong performance pay can induce distortion when the performance measure is imperfect — is formalized by Holmstrom and Milgrom (1991) and Baker (1992). In the context of public-sector corruption, the dependence of malfeasance on compensation structure is a classic result (Becker and Stigler 1974; Besley and McLaren 1993; Mookherjee and Png 1995).

    The contingency fee literature provides the specific institutional context. Dana and Spier (1993) and Rubinfeld and Scotchmer (1993) show that contingent fees can improve efficiency through screening and risk-sharing, but also create moral hazard. Garoupa (2008) shows that law firm organizational form responds to these incentive tensions. In our setting, the "benign" efficiency channel of contingency fees (better case selection, aligned effort) coexists with a "malign" channel: when the lawyer's pay is convex in the outcome, the marginal return to exploiting a judicial connection is high.

    The contingency fee channel also explains why connected plaintiff lawyers are more successful on observable margins (more cases, higher-value cases, larger clients): firms hiring plaintiff lawyers on contingency know that a connected lawyer delivers higher expected value, and are willing to pay for it.

    The market for connected lawyers

    <!-- slug: market-for-connections -->

    If connections generate rents, a market for connected representation should emerge. Firms seeking favorable outcomes will pay a premium for lawyers who are relatives of judges. This creates several equilibrium implications:

    • Sorting. Connected lawyers will handle disproportionately many cases in courts where their relative sits. The "home court advantage" reflects endogenous sorting, not random matching. Sytch and Kim (2021) document analogous venue shopping in patent litigation based on lawyer-judge connections.

    • Pricing. Connected lawyers can charge higher fees or attract higher-value cases, consistent with the OLS descriptive results (75% more cases, 21% higher face value, clients with 85% more employees). This parallels findings on the pricing of political connections in lobbying (Blanes i Vidal, Draca, and Fons-Rosen 2012) and corporate governance (Goldman, Rocholl, and So 2009).

    • Entry. Relatives of judges are drawn into legal practice at higher rates than the base population. The dynastic bias (~6x for lawyers, ~101x for judges) reflects both human capital transmission and the economic returns to connection (Laband and Lentz 1992; Lo Bello and Morchio 2022).

    The welfare implications depend on whether connected lawyers are also better lawyers (the human capital channel) or whether the premium reflects pure rent extraction. The Garoupa et al. finding that relatives of judges perform better on anonymous exams suggests some human capital component. Our causal estimates — which hold case characteristics fixed via random assignment — isolate the favoritism channel net of any skill differences. The distinction between know-how and know-who is formalized by Liu, Peng, Wang, and Xu (2025) in the context of China's revolving-door lawyers, and by Bertrand, Bombardini, and Trebbi (2014) for lobbying more broadly.

    Recusal rules as incomplete contracts

    <!-- slug: incomplete-contracts -->

    Recusal (impedimento) rules are the institutional response to judicial conflicts of interest. In the Brazilian system, judges must recuse when a lawyer is a relative up to the third degree. These rules are incomplete in at least three ways:

    1. Colleague connections. A lawyer who is a colleague of a judge's relative — working in the same firm, or frequently co-counseling — is not covered by recusal rules (until the 2015 reform, and even then compliance is low). This is the largest category of connections in our data and shows substantial favoritism effects with minimal recusal. The gap is a textbook case of an incomplete rule (Kaplow 1992): the rule-drafter faced a trade-off between precision (covering all possible connections) and administrability (clear, verifiable categories), and chose a bright line that excludes the most economically important margin.

    2. Substitute judge spillovers. When a judge recuses, the case goes to the substitute judge in the same court unit. If the substitute is sympathetic to colleagues or aware of the connection, recusal may not eliminate bias — it merely redirects it. About half of recusals go to the substitute in the same unit.

    3. Enforcement. Recusal must be raised by a party, but the opposing party faces a repeated-game problem: challenging a judge's impartiality today may invite retaliation in future cases before the same judge. The judge can recognize impedimento ex officio, but has no incentive to do so. The result is systematic under-enforcement, especially for indirect connections. This is consistent with Thorley's (2023) field experiment showing that judges rarely recuse and lawyers rarely press for it, and with the broader finding that whistleblowing is undersupplied when the whistleblower bears costs (Dyck, Morse, and Zingales 2010). The theoretical framework of Polinsky and Shavell (2000) predicts exactly this failure: quasi-private enforcement breaks down when enforcement is costly to the enforcer.

    The incompleteness of recusal rules creates a gradient: direct relatives face near-complete recusal, colleagues of relatives face partial recusal, and more distant connections (school cohort, social ties) face none. This gradient is itself informative — it reveals the margin at which institutional constraints bind versus where favoritism operates freely.

    Middlemen and trust

    <!-- slug: middlemen-trust -->

    When direct connections are covered by recusal rules, intermediaries can sustain corruption through indirect channels. A "middleman" lawyer who has repeated interactions with both the firm and the judge can bridge the trust gap:

    • The firm trusts the middleman because of their ongoing professional relationship.
    • The middleman trusts the judge to reciprocate because of repeated judge-middleman interaction (possibly sustained by the middleman being a colleague of the judge's relative).
    • The judge trusts the middleman because the middleman's own reputation and future business depend on maintaining the relationship.

    This trilateral trust structure makes the connection self-enforcing without requiring direct contact between the judge and the firm — a relational contract in the sense of Baker, Gibbons, and Murphy (2002) and Levin (2003). Historical precedents for such reputation-bridging intermediation include Greif's (1993) Maghribi traders' coalition and the private judges of the Champagne fairs (Milgrom, North, and Weingast 1990). In contemporary settings, Bertrand et al. (2007) document empirically how brokers dominate informal channels for obtaining Indian driver's licenses, concentrating extralegal payments and reducing the need for direct citizen-bureaucrat contact. Hasker and Okten (2008) formalize how intermediation can increase corruption by lowering search, bargaining, and enforcement costs in illegal transactions, while Drugov, Hamman, and Serra (2014) show experimentally that brokers also reduce moral costs of corruption.

    It also makes detection harder: the middleman's involvement appears as ordinary legal representation. Contingency fee contracts for the middleman further simplify the arrangement — the middleman is paid conditional on winning, eliminating the need for explicit side payments.

    Welfare consequences

    <!-- slug: welfare -->

    Judicial favoritism has costs beyond the direct transfer between litigants:

    • Allocative inefficiency. If connected lawyers win cases they should lose, the legal system fails to enforce contracts and protect property rights efficiently (Acemoglu and Johnson 2005; Djankov et al.). Resources flow to litigants who can afford connected representation rather than to those with meritorious claims.

    • Deterrence distortion. If employers know that connected lawyers can reduce penalties for labor violations, the expected cost of violations falls, weakening labor law enforcement. Cahuc et al. (2024) and Ponticelli and Alencar (2016) show that judicial bias and enforcement quality have real effects on firm behavior, employment, and investment in closely related settings.

    • Access to justice. Workers who cannot afford connected lawyers face a systematically unfavorable judiciary. The connection premium is a regressive tax on access to justice — paid disproportionately by poorer, less-connected litigants. Greiner, Pattanayak, and Hennessy (2013) provide causal evidence that representation quality matters for case outcomes, implying that unequal access to connected lawyers amplifies disparities.

    • Institutional legitimacy. If the public perceives that justice depends on connections rather than merit, voluntary compliance with legal norms erodes. This is a social multiplier: each act of favoritism reduces the perceived returns to playing by the rules. Tyler (2003) shows that procedural justice perceptions causally shape cooperation with legal institutions; Feld and Frey (2002) and Luttmer and Singhal (2014) document how trust and "tax morale" respond to perceived institutional fairness.

    These costs must be weighed against possible benefits: if connected lawyers also have higher ability (the human capital channel), their involvement may improve case quality or reduce litigation costs. The appeal results — connected cases are more likely to be reversed on appeal — suggest that the favoritism channel dominates the human capital channel in our setting.

Contents

    Hypotheses

    Testable predictions connecting theory (docs/theory.md) to empirical evidence. Each hypothesis identifies which theoretical mechanism generates the prediction, what competing theories predict differently, and how our random-assignment design can adjudicate.

    How to read this document

    Each hypothesis entry contains:

    • Slug: Stable identifier for cross-referencing (use instead of H-numbers, which may change if hypotheses are reordered)
    • Theory: Which framework from theory.md generates the prediction
    • Prediction: What theory says should happen
    • Competing prediction: What alternative theories predict differently
    • Pre-existing evidence: What we already know (from our results or the literature)
    • Evidence strength: How well existing evidence supports/contradicts (Very strong / Strong / Moderate / Weak)
    • Empirical test: How the hypothesis can be adjudicated with our design
    • Data requirements: Which datasets and variables are needed

    Hypotheses are organized into four clusters aligned with the paper's contribution.

    Cluster A: Favoritism in case outcomes

    These test the core claim: being randomly assigned a connected judge causes favorable outcomes.

    H1: Connected judge assignment increases plaintiff awards

    • Slug: plaintiff-awards
    • Theory: Direct reciprocity / implicit bias — the judge favors the connected lawyer's client through higher awards, more favorable rulings, or faster scheduling
    • Prediction: Cases randomly assigned to a judge connected to the plaintiff lawyer receive higher value awarded, higher probability of plaintiff (partial) victory, and earlier first hearings
    • Competing prediction (human capital): If connected lawyers are simply better (Garoupa et al. on anonymous exam performance), the effect should operate through lawyer quality, not judge assignment. Under pure human capital, random assignment to the connected judge should not matter conditional on the lawyer being connected
    • Pre-existing evidence: 30–40% increase in value awarded to plaintiff; 20% earlier first hearings; effect on plaintiff win probability. Randomization test p=0.56 (balance confirmed)
    • Evidence strength: Very strong
    • Empirical test: y_i = α_{c(i),t(i)} + β · Connected_i + ε_i, with court × filing year FE and IPW. Separate regressions for plaintiff-side and defendant-side connections
    • Data requirements: Case outcomes (valor da causa, desfecho, duração, data da audiência), judge-lawyer family links, court assignment data

    H2: No effect for defendant-side connections

    • Slug: defendant-null
    • Theory: Incentive contracts mediate favoritism — defendant lawyers on fixed wages cannot monetize the connection, so neither side has incentive to activate it (Holmstrom and Milgrom 1991; Baker 1992)
    • Prediction: Being assigned a connected judge has zero or negligible effect on outcomes when the connected lawyer represents the defendant
    • Competing prediction (pure favoritism): If judges favor relatives regardless of incentives (familial obligation, implicit bias), the effect should appear on both sides equally
    • Competing prediction (defendant selection): If defendants can switch lawyers after learning the judge assignment, selection effects could mask a real effect
    • Pre-existing evidence: Near-zero estimated effects for defendant-side connections across all outcome variables
    • Evidence strength: Strong (but open question about defendant lawyer selection noted in thinking.md)
    • Empirical test: Same specification as H:plaintiff-awards, restricted to defendant-side connections. Test whether β ≈ 0 with sufficient power
    • Data requirements: Same as H:plaintiff-awards, plus information on timing of lawyer hiring relative to judge assignment

    H3: Effect is larger for close relatives than colleagues of relatives

    • Slug: connection-strength-gradient
    • Theory: Connection strength determines magnitude of favoritism — direct relatives (spouse, child, sibling) have stronger trust and reciprocity channels than colleagues (same firm, co-counsel)
    • Prediction: β_direct > β_colleague > 0. The gradient reflects both trust intensity and the recusal compliance differential (direct relatives trigger recusal more often, so the effect conditional on non-recusal may be amplified by selection)
    • Competing prediction (institutional channel): If the effect operates through institutional knowledge rather than personal trust (e.g., the connected lawyer simply knows how the judge thinks), the gradient should be weaker — colleagues who work closely together may have similar informational advantages
    • Pre-existing evidence: Heterogeneity analysis shows effects for both types but pattern is documented
    • Evidence strength: Moderate
    • Empirical test: Interact Connected_i with connection type indicators (direct relative vs colleague of relative vs cousin). Compare coefficients
    • Data requirements: Detailed connection type classification from family linkage data

    H4: Early scheduling reveals pre-decision favoritism

    • Slug: early-scheduling
    • Theory: If favoritism operates through soft channels (scheduling, evidentiary rulings) rather than only through the final decision, we should see effects on process variables that precede the hearing of arguments
    • Prediction: Connected cases get earlier first hearing dates, controlling for case characteristics. This occurs before the judge has heard the merits — ruling out "justified" differentiation based on case quality
    • Competing prediction (information channel): If connected lawyers are better at filing urgent motions or communicating case urgency, earlier scheduling could reflect legitimate informational advantages
    • Pre-existing evidence: 20% earlier first hearings for connected plaintiff lawyers
    • Evidence strength: Strong
    • Empirical test: Regress days-to-first-hearing on Connected_i with court × year FE. The pre-hearing timing makes information-based explanations less plausible
    • Data requirements: Hearing date data, filing date, case characteristics

    Cluster B: Recusal compliance and institutional rules

    These test whether recusal rules effectively prevent favoritism, and where they fail.

    H5: Judges recuse for direct relatives but not for colleagues of relatives

    • Slug: recusal-selective
    • Theory: Recusal rules as incomplete contracts (Kaplow 1992) — rules draw a bright line at direct kinship, leaving the economically more important margin (colleagues) unregulated. Enforcement depends on the opposing party raising the conflict (Polinsky and Shavell 2000), which faces repeated-game retaliation costs (Thorley 2023)
    • Prediction: Sharp drop in probability of the connected judge deciding the case when the connection is a direct relative (impedimento applies); minimal change when the connection is a colleague of a relative (impedimento does not apply or is weakly enforced)
    • Competing prediction (full compliance): If the 2015 CNJ Resolution and 2016 CPC reform effectively extended impedimento to colleagues, compliance should increase post-reform
    • Pre-existing evidence: 41–42pp drop for direct relatives; 29pp pre-2015, 16pp post-2015 for colleagues. Cousins (not covered): no change. The 2015 reform did not noticeably increase compliance for colleague connections
    • Evidence strength: Very strong
    • Empirical test: Regress probability of connected judge deciding the case on connection type × period interactions
    • Data requirements: Judge identity on final decision, connection type, case filing date relative to reforms

    H6: Recusal to substitute judge does not eliminate bias

    • Slug: substitute-judge-bias
    • Theory: Substitute judge spillovers — when the connected judge recuses, about half of cases go to the substitute judge in the same court unit, who may be sympathetic or aware of the connection
    • Prediction: Even after recusal, cases connected to the original judge show some residual favoritism effect if decided by the substitute in the same vara
    • Competing prediction: Recusal fully eliminates bias — the substitute has no connection and no reason to favor the connected lawyer
    • Pre-existing evidence: About half of recusals go to the substitute in the same unit (documented in institutions.md)
    • Evidence strength: Moderate (mechanism documented, but causal effect on outcomes after recusal not yet tested)
    • Empirical test: Compare outcomes of connected cases decided by substitute judge (after recusal) vs unconnected cases decided by substitute judge
    • Data requirements: Judge identity on decision, distinction between titular and substitute, recusal indicator

    H7: The 2015/2016 reforms did not reduce favoritism for colleague connections

    • Slug: reform-ineffective
    • Theory: Incomplete enforcement — the reforms expanded the formal scope of impedimento but did not change the enforcement mechanism (party must raise conflict, judge decides). Without changing enforcement costs, the rule change alone is insufficient (Edelstein 2024)
    • Prediction: No structural break in either recusal rates or case outcome effects for colleague connections around 2015/2016
    • Competing prediction (reform effectiveness): If the reform changed behavior through salience, social norms, or increased risk of disciplinary action, effects should decline post-reform
    • Pre-existing evidence: Recusal compliance for colleagues actually decreased post-2015 (29pp → 16pp). This is puzzling and may reflect compositional changes or strategic responses
    • Evidence strength: Strong (for recusal rates); Moderate (for outcome effects)
    • Empirical test: Estimate H:plaintiff-awards specification separately for pre- and post-reform periods, restricted to colleague connections. Test for structural break
    • Data requirements: Case filing dates, connection type, outcomes

    Cluster C: The market for connected lawyers

    These test whether connections generate a market — whether connected lawyers attract more and better cases because clients know connections deliver value.

    H8: Connected lawyers handle more cases, higher-value cases, and larger clients

    • Slug: lawyer-market-sorting
    • Theory: Market for connections (Blanes i Vidal et al. 2012) — if connections generate rents, clients will pay a premium for connected representation. This should manifest in observable sorting: connected lawyers attract more business, especially high-stakes business
    • Prediction: Connected lawyers have more cases, higher average valor da causa, and represent firms with more employees, controlling for lawyer demographics (age, education, gender) and municipality
    • Competing prediction (human capital): If relatives of judges are simply better lawyers (better training, knowledge, professional networks), the same pattern emerges without any favoritism. The sorting is efficient, not rent-seeking
    • Pre-existing evidence: 75% more cases, 21% higher face value, clients with 85% more employees (OLS descriptive regressions)
    • Evidence strength: Very strong (for the correlation); Weak (for the causal interpretation — cannot distinguish favoritism from human capital with OLS)
    • Empirical test: OLS: lawyer success measures on connection indicators, controlling for quadratic polynomials in age and schooling, gender, municipality FE. Not causal — descriptive comparison
    • Data requirements: RAIS (lawyer demographics), case data (number of cases, valor da causa), firm data (number of employees)

    H9: Connected lawyers sort into "home courts" where their relative sits

    • Slug: home-court-sorting
    • Theory: Endogenous sorting — connected lawyers concentrate practice in courts where the connection is active, creating a "home court advantage" (Sytch and Kim 2021)
    • Prediction: Disproportionate share of a connected lawyer's caseload is in the court where the connected judge sits, compared to nearby courts or to the distribution of unconnected lawyers
    • Competing prediction (geography): If relatives simply live near each other, geographic proximity explains the sorting without strategic forum selection
    • Pre-existing evidence: Home vs away comparison shows correct sign for plaintiff lawyers (0.128 log points) but imprecise
    • Evidence strength: Moderate
    • Empirical test: Compare caseload distribution of connected lawyers across courts, with lawyer FE. Alternatively: does the connected lawyer's share of cases in the connected judge's court exceed what geography alone predicts?
    • Data requirements: Lawyer-court-year case counts, lawyer home municipality, court location

    H10: Dynastic entry into the legal profession reflects economic returns to connection

    • Slug: dynastic-entry
    • Theory: Entry incentives — if connections generate rents, relatives of judges should enter legal practice at higher rates than the base population (Laband and Lentz 1992; Lo Bello and Morchio 2022)
    • Prediction: Children of judges become lawyers at rates far exceeding what human capital transmission alone would predict. The dynastic bias should be higher for judges in courts with less oversight or larger caseloads (where rents are higher)
    • Competing prediction (pure human capital): If relatives become lawyers because of genuine talent and family knowledge, the dynastic bias should not vary with rent-extraction opportunities
    • Pre-existing evidence: Dynastic bias ~6x for lawyers, ~101x for judges. 52% of active magistrates report relatives in private practice (Sadek 2006)
    • Evidence strength: Strong (for the descriptive fact); Moderate (for the rent-extraction interpretation)
    • Empirical test: Descriptive: compute dynastic bias measures by court characteristics. Not the focus of the paper but motivates the question
    • Data requirements: Intergenerational linkage data (RAIS family links)

    Cluster D: Quality and welfare

    These test whether favoritism produces inefficiency — whether connected cases are decided worse, and whether the system distorts access to justice.

    H11: Connected cases are more likely to be reversed on appeal

    • Slug: appeal-reversal
    • Theory: If favoritism means the judge rules in favor of the connected side when the merits do not support it, the decision should be of lower quality. Appeals courts, which are not connected to the lawyer, should reverse these decisions at higher rates
    • Prediction: Cases decided by a connected judge in favor of the connected lawyer's client have higher reversal rates on appeal than comparable cases decided by unconnected judges
    • Competing prediction (human capital): If connected lawyers are better and bring stronger cases, their cases should have equal or lower reversal rates
    • Competing prediction (selection into appeal): If only the strongest cases of favoritism are appealed (because the losing side knows the decision was biased), the observed reversal rate overstates the quality gap in non-appealed cases
    • Pre-existing evidence: Appeal outcomes suggest lower quality decisions when connected judge decides
    • Evidence strength: Moderate (direction consistent with favoritism, but selection into appeal complicates interpretation)
    • Empirical test: Regress appeal outcome (reversed/upheld) on Connected_i for cases that are appealed. Address selection using Heckman-type correction or bounds
    • Data requirements: Appeal data linked to first-instance cases, appeal outcomes

    H12: Favoritism operates through the value awarded, not through the probability of plaintiff winning

    • Slug: value-vs-win-margin
    • Theory: Discretionary margins — judges have more discretion over the value of awards than over the binary win/lose decision. Favoritism should be concentrated where discretion is highest
    • Prediction: Large effect on valor awarded conditional on plaintiff winning, smaller effect on the probability of winning itself
    • Competing prediction: If favoritism is about ruling for the connected side regardless of merit, the effect should be strongest on the win/lose margin
    • Pre-existing evidence: Results show effects on both margins but the value channel appears dominant
    • Evidence strength: Moderate
    • Empirical test: Decompose the effect into extensive margin (probability of winning) and intensive margin (value conditional on winning)
    • Data requirements: Case outcomes with both binary and continuous measures

    H13: Connections have larger effects in high-value cases

    • Slug: value-interaction
    • Theory: If connections generate rents that must be shared between judge and lawyer, the incentive to activate the connection is proportional to the stakes. The marginal return to favoritism is higher in high-value cases (contingency fee is proportional to award)
    • Prediction: β is increasing in valor da causa. The interaction Connected × HighValue should be positive
    • Competing prediction (fixed cost): If activating the connection has a fixed cost (reputational risk, effort), the effect should be concentrated in high-value cases where the fixed cost is worth paying
    • Pre-existing evidence: Connected lawyers represent firms with 85% more employees and handle 21% higher face value cases — consistent with sorting into high-stakes litigation
    • Evidence strength: Moderate (sorting evidence is consistent, but direct test of interaction not yet reported)
    • Empirical test: Interact Connected_i with valor da causa (or deciles thereof). Test whether the treatment effect is increasing in case value
    • Data requirements: Valor da causa, case outcomes

    H14: The effect is a lower bound on total favoritism

    • Slug: lower-bound
    • Theory: Connected lawyers may benefit from both connected and unconnected judges. If unconnected judges in the same court are colleagues of the connected judge, they may also show mild favoritism (through professional courtesy, information sharing, or direct requests). Our estimand compares connected vs unconnected judge assignment, understating the total advantage of being a connected lawyer
    • Prediction: The OLS descriptive premium (75% more cases, 21% higher value) exceeds what the causal estimate (30-40% higher awards from connected judge) can explain. The gap reflects the unmeasured advantage with unconnected judges
    • Competing prediction: If the OLS premium is fully explained by human capital differences, the causal estimate is the correct measure of favoritism and the OLS captures selection, not additional bias
    • Pre-existing evidence: The OLS premium substantially exceeds the causal effect. Thinking.md flags this as an open question
    • Evidence strength: Weak (suggestive but hard to test directly)
    • Empirical test: Compare implied career premium from β (causal effect × share of cases with connected judge) to the observed OLS career premium. The gap is an upper bound on the unmeasured favoritism channel
    • Data requirements: Case-level treatment effects and lawyer-level career outcomes

    Cluster E: Mechanisms — how relatives are used to influence the judge

    A taxonomy of channels through which family connections facilitate judicial favoritism, ordered from most direct to most indirect. Each mechanism has different trust requirements, incentive structures, and empirical signatures. The anecdotal evidence from corruption scandals helps assess which are most plausible and prevalent.

    Mechanism taxonomy

    M1: Direct — relative is the lawyer on the case

    The simplest form. The judge's relative (spouse, child, sibling) is formally the lawyer of record on the case. The judge rules in favor of the relative's client.

    • Impedimento status: Triggers impedimento for relatives up to 3rd degree. Should not occur if rules are followed
    • Trust requirement: Minimal — the reciprocity is immediate and visible
    • Incentive contract: The relative is on contingency or receives honorários directly. Perfect alignment
    • Anecdotal evidence: Documented but rare at first instance because impedimento rules apply. At STJ: "três ex-corregedores caught voting on cases where their children were lawyers — blamed system failures" (institutions.md). Sadek (2006): 52% of magistrates have relatives in private practice. Operação Habeas Pater (TRF-1, 2023): desembargador Cândido Ribeiro (ex-president of TRF-1) and his son Ravik (lawyer) suspected of selling habeas corpus decisions to drug traffickers — R$270K cash found at son's home. The operation name itself references the father-son relationship. Operação Anaconda (TRF-3, 2003): judges' ex-wife served as "administradora dos ganhos" (R$550K cash found), sister was ex-auditor at Receita, niece was PF delegate suspected of aiding scheme. Intercept/CNJ data (2019): among 21 individual venda de sentenças cases since 2005, at least 3 involved family: son delivered R$400K bribe in TJ-BA; son participated in liminar scheme in RN; bribe transferred via sister's bank account in Pará
    • Testable prediction: Cases where the relative IS the lawyer should show recusal. When recusal fails, effects should be largest (but this is the margin most constrained by rules)
    • Our data: This is partially what we measure for "direct relative" connections. Recusal rate is 41–42pp (high but not complete)

    M2: Colleague channel — hire someone from the relative's firm

    The firm hires a lawyer who works in the same escritório as the judge's relative. The relative does not sign petitions and may not appear in the case file at all. The relative benefits through profit sharing, partnership income, or implicit reciprocity.

    • Impedimento status: Not covered until 2015 CNJ Resolution; CPC art. 144 VIII tried to extend coverage but STF struck it down in 2023 (7-4). Currently no formal impedimento
    • Trust requirement: Low-medium. The firm structure creates automatic benefit-sharing. The hiring firm need not coordinate directly with the judge
    • Incentive contract: The colleague lawyer may be on contingency (aligning incentives with the connected client). The relative benefits through firm profits regardless of formal case involvement
    • Anecdotal evidence: Most explicitly documented mechanism. Migalhas (post-STF 2023): "a contratação de determinado escritório muitas vezes é justamente influenciada por conter em seu quadro societário parente de algum juiz, ainda que ele não atue diretamente no seu caso." And: "é só o outro advogado(a) assinar a peça para que não ocorra o impedimento." Gilmar Mendes / Sérgio Bermudes case: Gilmar's wife is partner at Bermudes's firm; firm represented Eike Batista; Gilmar refused to recuse from Eike's habeas corpus
    • Testable prediction: This IS our main "colleague of relative" channel. Effect should be positive, recusal should be low (16pp post-2015). Effect may be smaller than M1 because the connection is weaker, but it operates on a much larger sample because recusal doesn't constrain it
    • Our data: This is what we measure. ~200K cases with colleague connections; low recusal; 30–40% effect on plaintiff awards

    M3: Parente-lobista — relative as partner/door-opener without caseload

    The relative joins a law firm as sócio or associado but does not carry a personal caseload at the court. Instead, they use access and familiarity with the judge to attract clients and influence outcomes informally — "acts as lobbyist in corridors" (institutions.md Scheme 3).

    • Impedimento status: Not covered if relative doesn't sign petitions. The 2022 law (signed by Bolsonaro) worsened this by allowing legal consultation "verbally or in writing, at the discretion of the lawyer and client, without requiring a formal mandate" — enabling invisible involvement
    • Trust requirement: Low. The firm benefits from the relative's name on the letterhead and their informal access. The relative benefits from partnership income
    • Incentive contract: The relative may receive a fixed partnership draw or a share of the firm's contingency fees. No direct case-level contract needed
    • Anecdotal evidence: Institutions.md explicitly lists this as Scheme 3. BBC article quotes: "Se passar a existir na sociedade a sensação de que só se ganham casos quem contrata escritório de parente, aí você tem um problema gigantesco." Top 6 law firms: partners 2x more likely to be judge relatives; employees 5x more likely; children 8x more likely
    • Testable prediction: Firms with a connected partner (identifiable via CNPJ sócios data) should have disproportionately high caseloads in the connected judge's court. The relative's name should appear infrequently in case records despite being a firm partner
    • Our data: Partially testable with CNPJ sócios data (available 2017+, has data de entrada na sociedade). Limited by 14% firm-matching rate. Could compare firms before/after adding a connected sócio
    • Distinction from M2: In M2, the relative is a working lawyer who happens to be a colleague. In M3, the relative is specifically recruited for their connection, not their legal skills

    M4: Cross-case quid pro quo — hire relative in unrelated case

    The firm hires the judge's relative as lawyer in a DIFFERENT case (unrelated to the focal case before the judge). The implicit expectation: the judge will favor the firm in the focal case in return for the business given to the relative.

    • Impedimento status: Not covered at all — the relative is not involved in the case before the judge
    • Trust requirement: HIGH. Neither party can write a formal contract linking the two cases. The exchange depends on repeated interaction and trust. From new_specification.org: "Requires firm to trust judge" (if relative hired before decision) or "Requires judge to trust firm" (if hired after as reward)
    • Incentive contract: No incentive contract is possible for the focal case, since the relative works on a different case. The arrangement is sustained by relational contracts (Baker, Gibbons, Murphy 2002). Contingency fees on the unrelated case partially align incentives but imperfectly
    • Anecdotal evidence: new_specification.org describes this as Specification 1. The event plot shows "large increase in hiring of relative of relator between sentença and acórdão — only of relator, not other desembargadores." This is suggestive but not statistically significant in regressions (driven by few cases). The TJ-MS scandal documents direct examples: firms gave business to judges' children in exchange for favorable decisions by those judges, but the children's cases were formally unrelated
    • Testable prediction: Being randomly assigned a judge should increase the probability that the firm hires that judge's relative in a DIFFERENT case, conditional on the firm having any other pending litigation. The timing should cluster around the focal case decision
    • Our data: Partially tested (Specification 1 in methods.md). Event plot is suggestive but imprecise. More power might come from a larger sample or a more sensitive test
    • Who facilitates this: The middleman (new_specification.org): "A middleman lawyer with repeated interactions both with firm and judge can create trust." The middleman can be on a contingency contract for the focal case, eliminating the firm's need to trust the judge directly

    M5: Esquema cruzado — judges' children practice before each other's parents

    Children of Judge A practice as lawyers before Judge B, and B's children practice before A. Neither child appears before their own parent (avoiding impedimento). The reciprocal benefit is sustained by mutual understanding — each judge favors the other's children, knowing their own children receive the same treatment.

    • Impedimento status: Not covered — the child is not practicing before their own parent
    • Trust requirement: Medium. Sustained by repeated observation: each judge can verify that the other is reciprocating by checking their children's success rates. Punishment for defection is withdrawal of reciprocity
    • Incentive contract: Children are typically on contingency fees. The cross-family arrangement creates aligned incentives without requiring explicit coordination
    • Anecdotal evidence: This is the defining feature of the TJ-MS Última Ratio scandal. CNJ: "verdadeiro consórcio entre desembargadores e seus filhos advogados, cujos interesses eram inegavelmente prestigiados, de maneira cruzada." Sideni ruled for Vladimir's children; Vladimir's children maintained financial relationships with Sideni (R$112K "cattle transaction" with fractionated cash). Offices of children of different desembargadores were in the same building. Intercepted: "Do Sideni também tem e... só que sempre pelos filhos, sabe? Sempre pelos filhos." Also documented in TJ-BA Faroeste: sons Arthur Barata and Ivanilton Junior (children of different desembargadores) collaborated as cross-family brokers
    • Cross-country evidence: Mujica (2011), in an ethnographic study of corruption at Peru's Palace of Justice, documents the identical mechanism: judges engage in reciprocal exchanges (trueque) specifically designed to circumvent anti-nepotism laws. The trueque maps directly onto the Brazilian esquema cruzado. Mujica also finds that bribes can only flow through trusted personal networks — family and university friendships are essential "capital" — and that the network structure is horizontal (no single boss), with shifting alliances sustained by mutual complicity. See docs/literature/lit_mujica2011.md
    • Testable prediction: Children of Judge A should have higher success rates before Judge B (where B is in the same court as A) than before judges in courts where A does not sit. The effect should be symmetric: A's children benefit before B's court AND B's children benefit before A's court
    • Our data: Testable if we can identify parent-child linkages specifically (subset of family data). Need to restrict to courts with multiple judges who both have lawyer-children. Small sample but very clean mechanism test
    • Physical colocation as signature: Children of different judges sharing office space (same building) is an additional testable indicator of coordination

    M6: Retainer contract with relative's firm

    A party (typically a large firm or bank) enters into a broad, non-specific retainer with the judge's relative's law firm. The retainer pays a monthly fee for "broad representation" without tying payment to specific cases. This creates financial dependence between the relative and the party, which the judge is aware of.

    • Impedimento status: Not covered unless the relative is formally on a specific case
    • Trust requirement: Low. The retainer creates a stable financial relationship that doesn't require case-by-case coordination. The party benefits from general goodwill; the judge knows the relative's livelihood depends on the client
    • Incentive contract: Fixed monthly retainer (not contingency). This is the model documented in the Banco Master / Viviane Barci scandal: R$3.6M/month for "broad representation" with no specific cases listed
    • Anecdotal evidence: The Banco Master case is the paradigmatic example. R$129M contract with Viviane Barci (wife of STF Minister Alexandre de Moraes) for "ampla representação." Also: Lewandowski's firm received consulting fees from Master during his quarentena. Multiple STF ministers' relatives have financial relationships with litigants who appear before the court
    • Testable prediction: More relevant for higher courts (STF/STJ) than for first-instance labor courts. In our setting, testable if we can identify retainer relationships through CNPJ sócios or RAIS employment data. Firms with retainer relationships with connected firms should have better outcomes before the connected judge
    • Our data: Limited — we don't observe retainer contracts. But RAIS could identify employment relationships between firms and law offices

    M7: Strategic addition of connected sócio to existing firm

    A law firm strategically adds a judge's relative as partner (sócio) to gain advantage in that judge's court. The relative may bring few cases but their name signals access to potential clients.

    • Impedimento status: Not covered (same as M3)
    • Trust requirement: Minimal for the firm — the relative is recruited specifically for the connection
    • Incentive contract: Partnership income. The relative benefits from the firm's increased business
    • Anecdotal evidence: CNPJ sócios data has data de entrada na sociedade, enabling event studies. Top-firm statistics: partners in top 6 ranked firms are 2x more likely to be judge relatives
    • Testable prediction: After a judge's relative joins a firm as sócio, the firm's caseload in that judge's court should increase. Event study around the data de entrada
    • Our data: CNPJ sócios data (2017+). Limited by matching rate but testable as an event study

    M8: Social/professional access — informal influence without formal role

    The relative does not practice law at all but uses family access to the judge to informally influence decisions. This includes passing information about cases, relaying preferences about scheduling, or simply signaling to the judge which cases matter.

    • Impedimento status: Not covered — no legal involvement at all
    • Trust requirement: Very high — the relative must be willing to act as go-between, and the communication is entirely informal
    • Incentive contract: None formal. The relative may receive gifts, lifestyle support, or other non-monetary benefits
    • Anecdotal evidence: The Zampieri phone messages show desembargador Sebastião de Moraes Filho texting condolences to the murdered lawyer — suggesting regular informal communication. STJ scandal: assessors (not always relatives, but sometimes family members) passed draft decisions to lobbyists via WhatsApp
    • Testable prediction: Hard to test directly with case data. May show up as: connected cases having outcomes that are MORE favorable than what the colleague or firm-level connection alone would predict
    • Our data: Not directly testable

    Which mechanisms drive our estimates?

    Our main results (30–40% effect for plaintiff-side connections, concentrated on colleague-of-relative connections) are most consistent with M2 (colleague channel) as the primary mechanism. The key evidence:

    1. Volume: 84% of connections in our data are colleague connections (M2), not direct relatives (M1)
    2. Recusal pattern: M1 is constrained by impedimento (41pp recusal); M2 operates freely (16pp recusal post-2015)
    3. Contingency fee asymmetry: M2 naturally accommodates the plaintiff-defendant asymmetry — the colleague lawyer is on contingency, creating aligned incentives. M4 (cross-case) cannot produce this asymmetry because the incentive contract is on a different case
    4. Scale: M2 operates at the level of individual cases, requiring no cross-case coordination (unlike M4) and no cross-judge reciprocity (unlike M5)

    However, our estimates may also capture some of M3 (parente-lobista) and M4 (cross-case quid pro quo) to the extent that these correlate with colleague connections. M5 (esquema cruzado) is testable separately as a heterogeneity analysis.

    M9: Reputation shield / deterrence of opposing party

    The party hires a connected lawyer not to activate favoritism but to deter the opposing side. The opposing lawyer, knowing the connection exists, advises their client to accept a worse settlement or moderate their litigation strategy. The judge may be entirely unaware.

    • Impedimento status: N/A — no judicial action needed
    • Trust requirement: None. The connection is public information (surname, known association). No judge-lawyer coordination needed
    • Conscious corruption: No. Pure belief-driven mechanism on the opposing side
    • Anecdotal evidence: Thorley (2023) shows lawyers rarely press for recusal — consistent with opposing counsel pulling punches. The "shadow of the connection" shifts the bargaining range even pre-trial
    • Testable prediction: The effect should appear on settlement outcomes and pre-hearing withdrawals, not only on judicial decisions. If connected cases settle more favorably for the connected side (even when the case never reaches the judge), this mechanism is operating
    • Distinction from M1–M8: All other mechanisms require the judge to act differently. M9 requires only that the opposing party believes the judge will act differently

    M10: Procedural information advantage (know-how from family proximity)

    The connected lawyer has superior knowledge of the judge's procedural preferences, scheduling patterns, evidentiary standards, and decision-making tendencies — not from corrupt communication but from family proximity (dinner conversations, growing up in a judicial household, absorbing judicial culture). They know which arguments resonate, which motions will be granted, when the judge is under time pressure.

    • Impedimento status: N/A — no formal conflict
    • Trust requirement: Minimal. Information flows passively through shared social context
    • Conscious corruption: No. The judge may never consciously share case-relevant information
    • Anecdotal evidence: Liu et al. (2025) decompose the revolving-door advantage into "know-how" vs "know-who." Conjur (2014): "Parentes de juízes têm relações profissionais ambíguas"
    • Testable prediction: Connected lawyers should make fewer procedural errors — lower rates of motions denied, fewer filings dismissed as untimely. The effect should be stronger with close relatives (more family exposure) than colleagues. Could test: does a connected lawyer's procedural success rate differ before the connected judge vs other judges?
    • Distinction from M1–M8: This is a human-capital channel acquired through the family relationship, not through professional merit. It's "know-how" but the source of the know-how is the connection

    M11: Court staff intermediation

    The judge's relative cultivates relationships with court staff (assessores, escrivães, oficiais de justiça) who provide informational advantages: advance notice of case assignments, scheduling flexibility, priority processing. In Brazilian labor courts, the assessor often drafts the initial decision text. Staff may act out of social obligation to the judge's family without the judge's knowledge.

    • Impedimento status: N/A — the staff member is the intermediary, not the judge
    • Trust requirement: Low-medium. Family connection facilitates relationships with staff
    • Conscious corruption: Not necessarily by the judge. Staff act from social obligation
    • Anecdotal evidence: Faroeste: niece created "association of servidores" as a channel. TJ-MS: assessor "Marcelo" had desembargador's password and signed decisions. STJ: assessors leaked draft decisions to lobbyists. Institutions.md: "links between lawyers and funcionários in the judiciary"
    • Testable prediction: Connected cases should show faster administrative processing (time from filing to distribution, time from decision to notification) independent of the judge's decision itself
    • Our data: Partially testable with audiência designada vs realizada dates and other processing timestamps

    M12: Credibility halo / implicit bias

    The judge unconsciously assigns higher credibility to the connected lawyer's arguments because of in-group affinity and familiarity. The judge processes the connected lawyer's brief more charitably, gives more time to present arguments, is more willing to accept factual claims, or interprets ambiguous evidence favorably. This is pure implicit bias (Verhagen and Yam 2021) — the judge genuinely believes they are being impartial.

    • Impedimento status: N/A — the judge is unaware of the bias
    • Trust requirement: None. Automatic
    • Conscious corruption: No
    • Anecdotal evidence: The appeal reversal result (connected cases more likely reversed) is consistent: the trial judge applies a lower evidentiary standard that a fresh appellate panel corrects
    • Testable prediction: Decision text should show fewer demands for additional evidence, fewer clarification requests, shorter deliberation in connected cases. The effect should not differ between cases where the lawyer actively invokes the connection and cases where they don't (because the bias is unconscious)
    • Distinction from M1–M8: All other mechanisms involve some action by the connected lawyer or third party. M12 operates through the judge's cognitive processing alone

    M13: Opposing counsel soft recusal / under-litigation

    The opposing lawyer, knowing they face a connected lawyer, pulls punches — refrains from challenging evidence, avoids aggressive motions, declines to request recusal. This is rational in a repeated game: being adversarial to a judge's relative creates reputational costs in a small legal community. The judge need not act differently; the opposing lawyer's strategic retreat shifts outcomes.

    • Impedimento status: N/A
    • Trust requirement: None between judge and connected lawyer
    • Conscious corruption: No
    • Anecdotal evidence: Thorley (2023): lawyers rarely press for recusal even when grounds exist. The repeated-game logic from Polinsky and Shavell (2000)
    • Testable prediction: Cases against connected lawyers should have fewer procedural motions filed by the opposing side, fewer objections, lower appeal rates. If opposing counsel systematically under-litigates, outcomes shift without judicial favoritism. Could test: does the opposing lawyer's motion-filing behavior differ when facing a connected vs unconnected lawyer?
    • Distinction from M9: M9 affects settlement behavior (pre-trial). M13 affects litigation conduct during the case (in-trial)

    M14: Post-decision reward / deferred compensation

    The party does NOT hire the connected lawyer before the case. After receiving a favorable decision, the party rewards the judge by subsequently hiring the judge's relative for future legal work. The judge acts first, trusting the party will reciprocate because the party has ongoing litigation needs.

    • Impedimento status: Not covered — no connection existed during the focal case
    • Trust requirement: Medium-high. The judge must trust the reward will come. Repeat litigants (large firms with many cases) are more trustworthy
    • Conscious corruption: Yes by the judge
    • Anecdotal evidence: new_specification.org Scheme 2: "relative hired after final decision as a reward to judge. Requires judge to trust firm." Event plot shows "large increase in hiring of relative of relator between sentença and acórdão"
    • Testable prediction: Parties who received favorable outcomes from Judge X should subsequently hire Judge X's relative at higher rates than parties who received unfavorable outcomes. The effect should be strongest for repeat litigants (who have future cases to offer)
    • Our data: Partially tested (event plot is suggestive but imprecise). Could extend with firm-level panel data

    M15: Lawyer quality signal / Bayesian updating by judge

    The judge observes that a connected lawyer has taken the case and rationally infers it has merit — because connected lawyers can cherry-pick winning cases. The judge applies a more favorable prior, shifting discretionary margins. This is not corruption but rational inference from a credible signal.

    • Impedimento status: N/A
    • Trust requirement: None
    • Conscious corruption: No — rational Bayesian updating
    • Testable prediction: The effect should be weaker in courts with high caseloads where the judge cannot track individual lawyers. It should disappear in cases where the connected lawyer took the case before it was assigned to the connected judge (eliminating the selection signal). Our random assignment design partially addresses this, but M15 could explain part of the residual
    • Distinction from pure human capital: In M15, the connected lawyer may not actually be better — they just signal quality through their willingness to take the case. The bias comes from the judge's inference, not from the lawyer's ability

    M16: Network reciprocity / diffuse judicial norms

    Broader than M5 (bilateral esquema cruzado). Judges within a court or TRT share a diffuse norm: "be reasonable toward colleagues' relatives, and they will be reasonable toward yours." No bilateral agreement is needed — the small, closed community of judges sustains generalized reciprocity (analogous to Putnam's social capital or Fisman and Miguel 2007 on diplomatic parking norms).

    • Impedimento status: Not covered
    • Trust requirement: Low — sustained by community norms, not bilateral agreements
    • Conscious corruption: Semi-conscious. Judges frame it as "professional courtesy" rather than corruption. "I would want the same for my child"
    • Testable prediction: The effect should be stronger in smaller courts/TRTs (where judges know each other, norms are stronger) and in courts where judge tenure overlap is longer. This predicts H:court-size-heterogeneity through a specific mechanism
    • Our data: Testable by interacting treatment with court size and judge tenure overlap

    Cluster E: Strategic behavior and mechanisms

    These test how parties respond to connections — whether litigants, lawyers, and firms act strategically around the connection.

    H15: Defendants switch lawyers after learning the connected judge

    • Slug: defendant-lawyer-switching
    • Theory: If the judge assignment is revealed before the defendant responds, defendants may strategically hire a connected lawyer to counteract the plaintiff's advantage. This is a form of endogenous treatment that could bias our estimates toward zero for defendant-side connections
    • Prediction: Defendants are more likely to hire a connected lawyer (or switch to one) in cases assigned to a judge who is a relative of a lawyer in the court. The probability of defendant-side connection increases with the judge assignment, conditional on court × year FE
    • Competing prediction: If defendant lawyers are hired before the judge is known (or if switching costs are high), there should be no correlation between judge assignment and defendant lawyer identity
    • Pre-existing evidence: Second randomization test shows no significant effect for plaintiffs, marginal effect for defendants (thinking.md flags this)
    • Evidence strength: Weak (suggestive but not yet fully explored)
    • Empirical test: Regress indicator for "defendant lawyer is connected to assigned judge" on assigned judge identity, with court × year FE. If positive, this indicates strategic lawyer selection by defendants
    • Data requirements: Timing of lawyer entry relative to judge assignment, lawyer identity, connection indicators

    H16: Firms hire connected lawyers for high-stakes cases (within-firm variation)

    • Slug: within-firm-allocation
    • Theory: If firms understand the value of connected representation, they should allocate connected lawyers to their most important cases. A firm using multiple lawyers should route high-value or high-risk cases to the connected one
    • Prediction: Within the same firm's litigation portfolio, cases handled by the connected lawyer have higher valor da causa than cases handled by unconnected lawyers from the same firm
    • Competing prediction (specialization): Connected lawyers may specialize in certain case types that happen to be higher-value, without any strategic allocation by the firm
    • Pre-existing evidence: Connected lawyers represent firms with 85% more employees — consistent with selection, but between-firm, not within-firm
    • Evidence strength: Not tested
    • Empirical test: Firm FE regressions: within the same firm, compare valor da causa of cases assigned to connected vs unconnected lawyers. Requires firms using multiple lawyers
    • Data requirements: Firm-lawyer-case linkage, valor da causa

    H17: Settlement rates differ for connected cases

    • Slug: settlement-effects
    • Theory: If both sides know the connected judge will favor the plaintiff, the settlement bargaining changes. The plaintiff's threat point is higher (expects a larger award at trial), so either: (a) settlements are larger to compensate, or (b) fewer cases settle because the plaintiff prefers to go to trial with a favorable judge
    • Prediction: Connected cases have either higher settlement values, lower settlement rates, or both — reflecting the connected side's stronger bargaining position (Spier 1994; Rickman 1999)
    • Competing prediction: If the connection is not common knowledge to both sides, settlement behavior is unaffected. Only the trial outcome changes
    • Pre-existing evidence: Not yet tested directly. 34% of cases settle overall
    • Evidence strength: Not tested
    • Empirical test: Regress settlement indicator and settlement value (when available) on Connected_i with court × year FE
    • Data requirements: Settlement indicator, settlement value (if recorded separately from court-awarded value)

    H18: Connected lawyers cluster in the same law firms (the "parente-lobista" firm structure)

    • Slug: parente-lobista-firms
    • Theory: The colleague-of-relative channel operates through law firms: a relative joins a firm not to practice independently but to attract clients who want access to the judge. The firm structure creates professional cover (Scheme 3 in institutions.md: "parente acts as partner/associate but does not sign petitions — acts as lobbyist in corridors")
    • Prediction: Law firms containing a judge's relative have higher caseloads in that judge's court than comparable firms without a connected partner. The relative's name may not appear on petitions — only the firm's other lawyers sign
    • Competing prediction: If relatives join firms for legitimate career reasons (human capital, mentorship), firm caseload distribution should not differ systematically
    • Pre-existing evidence: Migalhas article: "é só o outro advogado que atua no mesmo escritório em que o parente do juiz trabalha assinar a peça para que não ocorra o impedimento." Only 14% of connected lawyers linked to law firms in data, so matching is challenging
    • Evidence strength: Not tested (data limitation: firm-lawyer linkage is weak)
    • Empirical test: For firms with identifiable connected partners (via CNPJ sócios data), compare caseload in the connected judge's court vs other courts. Control for firm size and geography
    • Data requirements: CNPJ sócios data, lawyer-firm linkage, case-court-lawyer data

    H19: Effect stronger in courts with fewer judges (harder to avoid the connection)

    • Slug: court-size-heterogeneity
    • Theory: In small courts with 2–3 varas, a connected case has a 33–50% chance of landing with the connected judge; in large courts with 20 varas, only 5%. But large courts also offer more opportunities for sorting and manipulation. The institutional friction of randomization matters more where courts are small
    • Prediction: The treatment effect β is larger in smaller courts (fewer varas), because the connection is activated more frequently and the judge-lawyer relationship is more visible/intense
    • Competing prediction: If large courts have weaker randomization enforcement (thinking.md notes randomization fails in large courts), the effect could be larger there due to manipulation
    • Pre-existing evidence: Results robust to excluding large courts. Randomization test fails in courts with >20 units
    • Evidence strength: Not tested as explicit heterogeneity
    • Empirical test: Interact Connected_i with court size (number of varas). Exclude courts where randomization fails
    • Data requirements: Court size (number of varas), standard estimation data

    H20: Judge retirement (aposentadoria) reduces connected lawyer's success

    • Slug: judge-retirement
    • Theory: If the connected lawyer's advantage comes from the connection, it should evaporate when the connected judge retires. This is an event-study test analogous to Blanes i Vidal et al. (2012) on revolving-door lobbyists losing value when their connected politician leaves office
    • Prediction: Connected lawyers experience a decline in caseload, case values, or win rates after their connected judge retires, relative to a matched control group of unconnected lawyers
    • Competing prediction (human capital): If connected lawyers are simply good lawyers who happen to be related to judges, retirement of the judge should have no effect on their practice
    • Pre-existing evidence: Not yet tested. Thinking.md lists this as a "possible direction"
    • Evidence strength: Not tested
    • Empirical test: Event study around judge retirement date. Outcome: connected lawyer's quarterly caseload, valor da causa, or win rate. Use lawyer FE and compare to never-treated lawyers
    • Data requirements: Judge retirement dates (from RAIS or court records), lawyer-level panel of case outcomes

    H21: Esquema cruzado — children of different judges benefit from each other's parents

    • Slug: esquema-cruzado
    • Theory: Inspired by the TJ-MS scandal: judges' children don't practice before their own parent (which triggers impedimento) but before other judges in the same court. If these other judges reciprocate — favoring the colleague's children — a cross-family network emerges that circumvents recusal while maintaining mutual benefit
    • Prediction: A lawyer who is a child of Judge A performs better before Judge B (where B is a colleague of A in the same court) than before judges in courts where A does not sit. This is a "friend-of-friend" effect
    • Competing prediction: If the advantage is purely about knowledge of the court system (not about reciprocity), children of judges should perform equally well before all judges in the court, not just those who are colleagues of their parent
    • Pre-existing evidence: The TJ-MS Última Ratio scandal documents this pattern explicitly — Sideni ruled for Vladimir's children and vice versa. But we don't have statistical evidence at scale
    • Evidence strength: Not tested (anecdotal from scandals)
    • Empirical test: For lawyers who are children of judges: compare their outcomes before judges in the same court as their parent vs judges in other courts, using random assignment within court. Requires identifying children of judges specifically (not just any relative)
    • Data requirements: Parent-child linkage specifically (not just general family), judge-court assignments, case outcomes

    H22: Text analysis — connected decisions are qualitatively different

    • Slug: text-analysis
    • Theory: If favoritism operates through the decision itself (not just scheduling), connected decisions should differ textually: shorter reasoning, fewer citations, less engagement with the losing side's arguments, or more boilerplate language
    • Prediction: Decisions in connected cases (where the connected side wins) have shorter text, fewer legal citations, less discussion of evidence, or more formulaic language, compared to non-connected cases with similar outcomes
    • Competing prediction: If judges are careful to make connected decisions look indistinguishable (to avoid scrutiny), there should be no textual differences
    • Pre-existing evidence: Not tested. Thinking.md lists text analysis as a possible direction
    • Evidence strength: Not tested
    • Empirical test: NLP analysis of decision texts (length, citation count, sentiment, boilerplate detection). Regress text features on Connected_i with court × year FE
    • Data requirements: Full text of decisions (sentencas), NLP pipeline

    H23: Firms with connected representation have more labor violations

    • Slug: firm-violations
    • Theory: Deterrence distortion — if employers know that connected lawyers reduce expected penalties for labor violations, the expected cost of violations falls. Firms with access to connected lawyers should invest less in compliance, leading to more workplace violations or more labor court cases filed against them
    • Prediction: Firms that regularly use connected defendant lawyers have more labor court cases filed against them per employee, more MTE (Ministry of Labor) inspection violations, or higher worker turnover, compared to similar firms without connected representation
    • Competing prediction (reverse causation): Firms that violate more seek connected lawyers precisely because they expect litigation — the connection is a response to violation, not a cause
    • Pre-existing evidence: Not tested. Connected lawyers represent firms with 85% more employees (but this is selection, not deterrence)
    • Evidence strength: Not tested (and very hard to identify causally)
    • Empirical test: Descriptive: correlate firm-level violation rates with use of connected defendant lawyers. Causal identification extremely difficult — would need a shock to connection availability (e.g., judge retirement)
    • Data requirements: MTE inspection data, RAIS employer records, firm-lawyer linkage, case data

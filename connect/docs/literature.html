    Literature

    Judicial connections and corruption

    • Choi (2015)

      • topic: Judge connections to lawyers/parties
      • relevance: One of three papers on consequences of judicial connections

    • Conti and Valentini (2017)

      • topic: Judge connections to lawyers/parties
      • relevance: One of three papers on consequences of judicial connections

    • Liu and Zhang (2020)

      • topic: Judge connections to lawyers/parties
      • relevance: One of three papers on consequences of judicial connections; home vs away court comparison

    • Wang et al (Shaoda Wang)

      • topic: Home vs away court comparison for connected lawyers
      • relevance: Related identification strategy — comparing lawyer performance across courts with/without connections

    • Huang, Sterling, Roychowdhury, Sletten, Xu. "Just Friends? Managers' Connections to Judges." Journal of Accounting Research, 2024/2025

      • topic: Social connections between judges and corporate executives affect case outcomes
      • relevance: Very close. Direct analog: personal connection → judicial outcome, using random assignment. Useful for positioning our design and discussing favoritism vs information channels

    • Liu, Peng, Wang, Xu. "The Law and Economics of Lawyers: Evidence from the Revolving Door in China's Judicial System." BFI/NBER WP 33708, 2025

      • topic: Ex-judges turned lawyers ("revolving door") deliver 8–23% higher win rates
      • relevance: Very close. Decomposes know-how vs know-who; formalizes trade-off between quality improvement and bias from connections. Central reference for separating human capital from favoritism channel

    • Sytch and Kim. "Quo Vadis? From the Schoolyard to the Courtroom." Administrative Science Quarterly, 2021

      • topic: Educational/professional connections between patent lawyers and judges; venue shopping
      • relevance: Shows firms choose jurisdictions based on lawyer-judge connections; when connected judge is drawn, win rate rises. Highlights random assignment as key institutional friction

    • Verhagen and Yam. "The law of attraction: How similarity between judges and lawyers helps win cases in the Hong Kong Court of Final Appeal." International Review of Law and Economics, 2021

      • topic: Judge-lawyer similarity (education, workplace) and win probability
      • relevance: Bridge to discussion of affinity/credibility channel vs corruption. Motivates tests distinguishing favoritism from persuasion/quality

    • Metcalfe. "The Role of Courtroom Workgroups in Felony Case Dispositions." Law & Society Review, 2016

      • topic: Familiarity between judge, prosecutor, and defense affects plea deals and case duration
      • relevance: Repeated interactions create routines and relational power; motivates heterogeneity by "side" (plaintiff vs defendant)

    • Lu, Pan, Zhang. "Political Connectedness and Court Outcomes: Evidence from Chinese Corporate Lawsuits." Journal of Law and Economics, 2015

      • topic: Courts favor state-owned and politically connected firms
      • relevance: Connection-based bias in emerging economy courts; effect moderated by institutional quality

    • Zhang, Peng. "Anti-corruption campaign, political connections, and court bias." Journal of Public Economics, 2023

      • topic: Anti-corruption campaign reduces win-rate advantage of politically connected firms by 6.3%
      • relevance: Evidence that reforms can reduce connection-based bias; template for testing interaction between enforcement intensity and favoritism

    Nepotism and dynasties in public institutions

    • Dal Bo et al

      • topic: Dynastic bias in politics
      • relevance: Comparable dynastic bias measure; also has table similar to our table on dynasties

    • Riano (2022) "Bureaucratic Nepotism"

      • topic: Nepotism in bureaucracies
      • relevance: Related literature on family connections in public sector

    • George (2024)

      • topic: Legal dynasties
      • relevance: Dynastic bias in legal professions

    • Brassiolo et al (2021) "Family Rules: Nepotism in the Mexican Judiciary"

      • topic: Nepotism in Mexican courts
      • relevance: Closest comparable paper — nepotism among judges and judicial servants

    • Garoupa et al (via Nuno Garoupa email)

      • topic: Persistence of surnames in judiciary via specific human capital
      • relevance: Anonymous exam results show relatives of judges perform better — could reflect human capital rather than connections
      • link: https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?pid=S0718-090X2024005000102&script=sci_arttext

    • Almeida (2010/2014) "A Nobreza Togada"

      • topic: Legal elites in Brazil, historical analysis
      • relevance: Documents legal dynasties in Brazil since colonial period; shows pattern of nepotism in judiciary from monarchy to present
      • notes: docs/literature/lit_almeida2010.md
      • file: almeida2010nobreza.pdf

    • Costa de Oliveira (2014) "Politica, Direito, Judiciario e Tradicao Familiar"

      • topic: Politics, law, judiciary and family tradition in Brazil
      • relevance: Detailed case studies of judicial families in Parana

    • Leal (1977) "Coronelismo, Enxada e Voto"

      • topic: Classical account of Brazilian patronage politics (coronelismo) — reciprocal exchange between local landowners and state
      • relevance: Historical foundation for informal-exchange mechanisms that underpin connection-based favoritism in Brazil
      • notes: docs/literature/lit_leal1977.md
      • file: leal1977coronelismo.pdf

    • Mujica (2011) "Micropolíticas de la corrupción"

      • topic: Ethnography of corruption networks in Lima's Palace of Justice (Peru)
      • relevance: Documents the "trueque" cross-family exchange mechanism that mirrors the TJ-MS "esquema cruzado"; argues bribes flow only through trusted personal networks (family, university ties)
      • notes: docs/literature/lit_mujica2011.md
      • file: mujica2011micropoliticas.pdf

    • Vasconcelos (2005) "Juízes no banco dos réus"

      • topic: Book-length account of Operação Anaconda and related TRF-3 judicial corruption cases
      • relevance: Primary source for the Anaconda scandal and earlier venda-de-sentenças cases; documents family roles (ex-wives, siblings, in-laws) in managing proceeds
      • notes: docs/literature/lit_vasconcelos2005.md

    • Alvim (2011) "Operação Hurricane: um juiz no olho do furacão"

      • topic: Self-authored account by a TRF-2 desembargador charged in Operação Hurricane
      • relevance: Defendant's perspective on a judicial corruption case with family involvement; explicit passages on the prevalence of relatives practicing law before their desembargador family members
      • notes: docs/literature/lit_alvim2011.md

    • Da Ros & Taylor (2017) "Opening the Black Box: Three Decades of Reforms to Brazil's Judicial System"

      • topic: Institutional reforms of the Brazilian judiciary since redemocratization
      • relevance: Context on the CNJ, oversight mechanisms, and structural reform efforts relevant to interpreting corruption episodes and enforcement intensity
      • notes: docs/literature/lit_daros2017.md
      • file: daros2017opening.pdf

    • Fafchamps and Labonne. "Do Politicians' Relatives Get Better Jobs?" Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization, 2017

      • topic: Family connections to politicians and occupational outcomes in the Philippines
      • relevance: Classic identification strategy for nepotism using close elections; parallel to connection → public sector privilege

    • Gagliarducci and Manacorda. "Politics in the Family: Nepotism and the Hiring Decisions of Italian Firms." AEJ: Applied, 2020

      • topic: Political family connections affect firm hiring decisions
      • relevance: Separates rents from connections vs efficient talent allocation; heterogeneity by who gains most from connection

    • Sundell. "Nepotism and Meritocracy." QoG Working Paper, 2014

      • topic: Family ties coexisting with meritocratic recruitment
      • relevance: Nepotism manifests even in systems with formal merit rules — parallels our finding of compliance for direct relatives but not indirect connections

    • Castagnola and González-Ocantos. "Argentina's 'Judicial Family'." In Informality and Courts, 2025

      • topic: Family connections in the Argentine provincial judiciary
      • relevance: Regional comparator for judicial dynasties; typology of family ties relevant to our framework

    • Hassett and Randazzo. "Education versus Experience? An Elite Legal Dynasty among Federal Judges in the United States." In Informality and Courts, 2025

      • topic: Legal dynasty reproduction through credentials vs professional experience in US federal courts
      • relevance: Articulates how "colleagues of relatives" channel is a natural extension of dynasties

    • Dupraz and Simson. "Elite Persistence in Sierra Leone." Journal of Development Economics, 2024

      • topic: Surname-based elite persistence across generations
      • relevance: Methodological inspiration for surname/kinship measures; substantive evidence on institutional inequality reproduced through networks

    Judge behavior and decision-making

    • Cahuc, Carcillo, Patault, Moreau. "Judge Bias in Labor Courts and Firm Performance." JEEA, 2024

      • topic: Pro-labor judge bias via quasi-random assignment; heterogeneous effects on firm employment and survival
      • relevance: Central reference for labor court + random assignment identification; benchmark for magnitude and economic consequences of bias

    • Araujo, Ferreira, Lagaras, Moraes, Ponticelli, Tsoutsoura. "The Labor Effects of Pro-labor Bias in Bankruptcy." NBER WP 28640, 2021/2023

      • topic: Text-based pro-worker bias measure; random assignment of bankruptcy cases in São Paulo
      • relevance: Brazilian methodological benchmark — random case distribution, bias measurement, heterogeneity analysis
      • file: araujo2021labor.pdf

    • Ichino et al (2002)

      • topic: Local labor market conditions and labor court decisions
      • relevance: Context-dependent bias in labor courts

    • Marinescu (2011)

      • topic: Local labor market conditions and labor court decisions
      • relevance: Context-dependent bias in labor courts

    • Berger and Neugart (2011)

      • topic: Appointing government's effect on labor judge decisions
      • relevance: Political bias in labor courts
      • file: berger2011labor.pdf

    • Kling, Waldfogel

      • topic: Judge random assignment as identification strategy
      • relevance: Methodological foundation for our approach

    • Hernan (2010)

      • topic: Inverse probability weighting in causal inference
      • relevance: Methodological reference for IPW approach

    • Iaryczower

      • topic: Effect of deliberation on judicial panels
      • relevance: Related mechanism — how judges influence each other in panels

    • Eisenberg et al. "Does the Judge Matter? Exploiting Random Assignment on a Court of Last Resort." 2012

      • topic: Separating judge effects, panel composition, and case selection under random assignment
      • relevance: Methodological reference for validating randomization design and decomposing variation

    • Ganglmair. "Do Judicial Assignments Matter?" AEA Annual Meeting, 2024/2025

      • topic: Introduction of random assignment in patent courts that previously had case concentration
      • relevance: Random assignment as institutional policy instrument against judge shopping; even under formal randomization, networks can still generate power

    • Knill, Kindelsperger, Ovtchinnikov. "Stock Ownership of Federal Judges and its Impact on Corporations." WP, 2021

      • topic: Judges with industry stock holdings favor corporate litigants under random assignment
      • relevance: Measurable conflict of interest despite recusal rules — direct parallel to our finding of selective compliance

    • Johnson and Parton. "Judges Breaking the Law: An Empirical Study of Financially Interested Judges Deciding Cases." North Carolina Law Review, 2020

      • topic: Judges participating in cases despite financial conflicts
      • relevance: Documents systematic "nonrecusal" from weak self-enforcement — central to our argument about incomplete recusal compliance

    • Thorley. "The Failure of Judicial Recusal and Disclosure Rules: Evidence from a Field Experiment." Northwestern University Law Review, 2023

      • topic: Randomized field experiment testing recusal compliance when conflicts flagged
      • relevance: Key reference. Judges rarely recuse and lawyers don't press — directly parallels our finding of endogenous compliance, especially for indirect connections

    • Axbard. "Convicting Corrupt Officials: Evidence from Randomly Assigned Cases." Review of Economic Studies, 2025

      • topic: Random case assignment in Philippine anti-corruption court; convictions improve fiscal management
      • relevance: Judge characteristics identified by lottery have real and persistent impacts; developing-country example with explicit integrity-by-design
      • file: axbard2025convicting.pdf

    Political and social connections in economic outcomes

    • Fisman (2001)

      • topic: Political connections and firm value (Indonesia/Suharto)
      • relevance: Foundational paper on political connections; notes intertwining of connections with family ties
      • file: fisman2001estimating.pdf

    • Faccio. "Politically Connected Firms." American Economic Review, 2006

      • topic: Multi-country evidence that politically connected firms are common and receive economic benefits
      • relevance: Classic reference for connections as rent mechanism; compare institutional capture in judiciary vs markets

    • Khwaja and Mian. "Do Lenders Favor Politically Connected Firms?" QJE, 2005

      • topic: Connected firms get more credit but have higher default in Pakistan
      • relevance: Higher access + worse performance ex post = signature of favoritism, analogous to connected lawyers winning cases they should lose

    • Goldman, Rocholl, So. "Do Politically Connected Boards Affect Firm Value?" Review of Financial Studies, 2009

      • topic: Board political connections repriced around elections
      • relevance: Connections generate priceable rents; event-study analog to random judge assignment

    • Jayachandran. "The Jeffords Effect." Journal of Law and Economics, 2006

      • topic: Unexpected Senate control change reprices politically aligned firms
      • relevance: Connections have large, immediately capitalized value — justifies why agents invest in cultivating judicial networks

    • Zitzewitz (2012) "Forensic Economics"

      • topic: Distinguishing favoritism from corruption
      • relevance: Methodological framework for separating favoritism and corruption; our random assignment + family network design produces a "forensic" causal signature

    • Olken. "Monitoring Corruption: Evidence from a Field Experiment in Indonesia." JPE, 2007

      • topic: Randomized audits reduce measurable corruption in village road projects
      • relevance: Reference for indirect validation and how oversight reforms affect incentives for favoritism

    • Di Tella and Schargrodsky. "The Role of Wages and Auditing During a Crackdown on Corruption." Journal of Law and Economics, 2003

      • topic: Audits and institutional changes reduce corruption-consistent pricing in Buenos Aires hospitals
      • relevance: Corruption as observable price distortion in administrative data; parallel to interpreting awards as sensitive to favoritism

    • Fisman and Miguel. "Corruption, Norms, and Legal Enforcement: Evidence from Diplomatic Parking Tickets." JPE, 2007

      • topic: Diplomatic parking violations correlate with home-country corruption norms
      • relevance: Cultural/institutional mechanisms behind illicit behavior; heterogeneity prediction — connections matter more where enforcement is weaker

    Incentive contracts, collusion, and corruption

    • Holmstrom and Milgrom. "Multitask Principal–Agent Analyses." JLEO, 1991

      • topic: Strong incentives on measurable output distort effort away from unmeasured dimensions
      • relevance: Key. Theoretical basis for why contingent fees (pay-for-performance) can induce rent-seeking via connections while fixed wages do not

    • Baker. "Incentive Contracts and Performance Measurement." JPE, 1992

      • topic: Contracts based on imperfect performance measures distort agent behavior even under risk neutrality
      • relevance: Under contingency fees, the "score" (award) becomes the target; if connections alter the score, incentive to exploit connection arises endogenously

    • Becker and Stigler. "Law Enforcement, Malfeasance, and Compensation of Enforcers." Journal of Legal Studies, 1974

      • topic: Salary vs incentive pay for law enforcers and effects on malfeasance
      • relevance: Classic antecedent: compensation structure determines the set of viable opportunistic behaviors

    • Besley and McLaren. "Taxes and Bribery: The Role of Wage Incentives." The Economic Journal, 1993

      • topic: Tax inspector corruption under moral hazard; optimal wage/monitoring regimes
      • relevance: Private return to corruption depends on the contract — directly parallels contingency fee channel

    • Mookherjee and Png. "Corruptible Law Enforcers: How Should They Be Compensated?" The Economic Journal, 1995

      • topic: Strategic interaction between regulated agent and corruptible inspector; non-trivial punishment effects
      • relevance: Formal rules (recusal, sanctions) can fail or generate strategic responses; looking at "does rule exist?" is insufficient

    • Van Rijckeghem and Weder. "Bureaucratic Corruption and the Rate of Temptation." Journal of Development Economics, 2001

      • topic: Civil service wages and corruption cross-country
      • relevance: Plausibility: when marginal return to rent extraction is high relative to outside option, networks and favoritism are more attractive

    • Tirole. "Hierarchies and Bureaucracies: On the Role of Collusion in Organizations." JLEO, 1986

      • topic: Collusion within hierarchies as group phenomenon sustained by incentives and information
      • relevance: Theoretical basis for modeling judge-lawyer as collusion problem in public organization

    • Kofman and Lawarree. "Collusion in Hierarchical Agency." Econometrica, 1993

      • topic: Auditor-manager collusion under imperfect monitoring; limits of deterrence
      • relevance: Transferable to lawyer (as interested intermediary) colluding with judge; gain from collusion grows when lawyer pay is outcome-linked

    • Khalil and Lawarree. "Incentives for Corruptible Auditors in the Absence of Commitment." Journal of Industrial Economics, 2006

      • topic: Limited commitment reduces effectiveness of anti-corruption mechanisms
      • relevance: Institutional principal has commitment problems; repeated relationships create space for informal agreements — parallels weak recusal enforcement

    • Garoupa. "Why Large Law Firms Prefer Hourly Fees over Contingent Fees." JLEO, 2008

      • topic: Moral hazard and common agency in law firm partnerships; hourly fees as organizational solution
      • relevance: Organizational microfoundation for plaintiff-defendant asymmetry: when lawyer is "case entrepreneur" (contingency), connection becomes a rentable asset

    • Shleifer and Vishny. "Corruption." QJE, 1993

      • topic: Institutional structure determines level and cost of corruption; secrecy makes corruption more distortive than taxation
      • relevance: Networks can act as "property" over a public resource (judicial decision), enabling selective rent extraction even under randomization

    Relational contracts, repeated games, and intermediaries

    • Baker, Gibbons, Murphy. "Relational Contracts and the Theory of the Firm." QJE, 2002

      • topic: Self-enforcing agreements in repeated games; organizational form affects deviation temptation
      • relevance: Key. Backbone for modeling favoritism as relational contract between judge and lawyer that cannot be formally written or enforced

    • Levin. "Relational Incentive Contracts." AER, 2003

      • topic: Optimal incentive design when contracts are self-enforcing; continuation value disciplines behavior
      • relevance: Corruption only sustains if future surplus from repeated judge-lawyer-client interactions is sufficient to discipline deviations

    • Greif. "Contract Enforceability and Economic Institutions in Early Trade: The Maghribi Traders' Coalition." AER, 1993

      • topic: Reputation-based coalition enforcing agent-principal relations via multilateral punishment
      • relevance: Historical microfoundation for trust networks and the intermediary as reputation bridge — exactly the firm-lawyer-judge triangle

    • Milgrom, North, Weingast. "The Role of Institutions in the Revival of Trade." Economics and Politics, 1990

      • topic: Private institutions (including private judges, reputation mechanisms) sustain cooperation when state enforcement is weak
      • relevance: Informal arrangements coexist with and substitute for formal institutions at the margin — parallels favoritism despite formal recusal rules

    • Dixit. Lawlessness and Economics: Alternative Modes of Governance. Princeton, 2004

      • topic: Reputation, networks, intermediation, and private norms as governance beyond the state
      • relevance: Frames our setting: formal randomization and rules exist, but family/professional networks generate a parallel governance layer producing favoritism

    • Bertrand, Djankov, Hanna, Mullainathan. "Obtaining a Driver's License in India: An Experimental Approach to Studying Corruption." QJE, 2007

      • topic: Brokers/intermediaries dominate informal channels and concentrate extralegal payments
      • relevance: Key. Closest empirical parallel to middleman mechanism: client trusts intermediary who knows how to operate the network with the public agent

    • Hasker and Okten. "Intermediaries and Corruption." Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 2008

      • topic: Middlemen reduce search, bargaining, and enforcement costs in corrupt transactions
      • relevance: Formal model of how intermediation can increase corruption by making illegal agreements more viable — maps to firm-lawyer-judge triangle

    • Drugov, Hamman, Serra. "Intermediaries in Corruption: An Experiment." Experimental Economics, 2014

      • topic: Lab evidence that brokers reduce uncertainty and moral costs, increasing corruption
      • relevance: Micro-mechanisms (uncertainty reduction, moral distance, coordination) that can be mapped to empirical tests (e.g., effects larger where network is denser)

    • Olken and Pande. "Corruption in Developing Countries." Annual Review of Economics, 2012

      • topic: Survey of evidence and methods on corruption; strategic adaptation by agents
      • relevance: Methodological anchor for our "forensic" approach using institutional variation to identify favoritism signatures
      • file: olken2011corruption.pdf

    • Sequeira and Djankov. "Corruption and Firm Behavior: Evidence from African Ports." Journal of International Economics, 2014

      • topic: Firms endogenously adapt to corrupt environments, distorting logistics and production
      • relevance: Analog to deterrence distortion: if favoritism reduces expected penalties, firms adjust behavior (compliance, contestation, settlements) inefficiently

    Institutional design, recusal, and conflict of interest

    • Kaplow. "Rules Versus Standards: An Economic Analysis." Duke Law Journal, 1992

      • topic: Trade-offs between ex ante rule-drafting costs and ex post interpretation costs
      • relevance: Key. Recusal rules are a classic incomplete rule — cover direct relatives but not colleagues; Kaplow justifies why gaps exist and open space for exploitation

    • Maskin and Tirole. "The Politician and the Judge: Accountability in Government." AER, 2004

      • topic: Accountability vs delegation trade-offs; when judicial independence creates disciplining problems
      • relevance: Why recusal depends on external enforcement and why accountability may be insufficient to contain favoritism, especially for indirect/hard-to-observe conflicts

    • Aghion and Tirole. "Formal and Real Authority in Organizations." JPE, 1997

      • topic: Formal authority (right to decide) vs real authority (effective control driven by information)
      • relevance: Even when rules assign formal authority (judge must recuse), real authority lies with whoever controls information and enforcement triggers (parties/lawyers) — directly parallels our point about costly challenges

    • Polinsky and Shavell. "The Economic Theory of Public Enforcement of Law." JEL, 2000

      • topic: Probability of punishment, sanction magnitude, and division between public and private enforcement
      • relevance: Recusal with quasi-private enforcement (party must allege) fails when enforcement is costly — exactly our mechanism of retaliation in repeated games

    • Gibson and Caldeira. "Judicial Impartiality, Campaign Contributions, and Recusals." Journal of Empirical Legal Studies, 2013

      • topic: Survey experiment: campaign contributions erode perceived impartiality; recusal partially but not fully repairs legitimacy
      • relevance: Supports argument that favoritism costs welfare via legitimacy erosion; partial recusal doesn't neutralize perception damage

    • Edelstein. "Recusal as Remedy: Disincentivizing Donors." State Politics & Policy Quarterly, 2024

      • topic: Strict recusal rules can reduce expected return of influence-seeking connections
      • relevance: Recusal only "works" if it cuts the economic value of the conflict; if indirect connections are uncovered, the market migrates to the unregulated margin
      • file: edelstein2024recusal.pdf

    • Dyck, Morse, Zingales. "Who Blows the Whistle on Corporate Fraud?" Journal of Finance, 2010

      • topic: Enforcement often depends on actors with costs and risks to reveal; many governance mechanisms have weak whistleblowing incentives
      • relevance: Strong analogy to our enforcement problem: opposing party must challenge the judge but fears retaliation in repeated interactions

    Access to justice and inequality

    • Lichand and Soares. "Access to Justice and Entrepreneurship: Evidence from Brazil's Special Civil Tribunals." Journal of Law and Economics, 2014

      • topic: Expansion of small claims courts in São Paulo increases access and affects economic behavior
      • relevance: Institutional design (reach, cost) alters behavior beyond the courtroom; justifies why unequal access to connected lawyers has broad economic effects

    • Ponczek and Grezzana. "Gender Bias at the Brazilian Superior Labor Court." Brazilian Review of Econometrics, 2012

      • topic: Gender of judge/panel composition and outcomes in TST under random assignment
      • relevance: Direct antecedent in Brazilian labor courts with random assignment; motivates heterogeneity by side and incentives

    • Helland and Tabarrok. "Contingency Fees, Settlement Delay, and Low-Quality Litigation." JLEO, 2003

      • topic: Contingency fee caps affect case selection, settlement timing, and litigation quality
      • relevance: Key for incentive asymmetry. Lawyer fee structure alters strategic behavior; directly supports why connection effects concentrate on plaintiff-side contingency lawyers

    • Dana and Spier. "Expertise and Contingent Fees: The Role of Asymmetric Information in Attorney Compensation." JLEO, 1993

      • topic: Contingent fees align incentives under asymmetric information about case merit
      • relevance: Separates efficiency channel (screening/advice) from rent extraction; theoretical basis for distinguishing legitimate connected-lawyer advantage from judicial favoritism

    • Rubinfeld and Scotchmer. "Contingent Fees for Attorneys: An Economic Analysis." 1993

      • topic: Economic analysis of contingency fees under asymmetric information and risk
      • relevance: When contingent fees expand access vs generate distortions; connections matter more when fee is convex in outcome

    • Spier. "Settlement Bargaining and the Design of Damage Awards." JLEO, 1994

      • topic: Award design and litigation costs affect bargaining and case selection
      • relevance: Favoritism may operate via bargaining (credible threat of higher award when connected judge drawn) and via composition of cases reaching verdict

    • Rickman. "Contingent fees and litigation settlement." International Review of Law and Economics, 1999

      • topic: Contingent fee lawyers adopt hard bargaining; ambiguous effects on settlement
      • relevance: Fee structure amplifies bargaining power; connections amplify already-strong plaintiff-side incentives

    • Greiner, Pattanayak, Hennessy. "The Limits of Unbundled Legal Assistance: A Randomized Study." Harvard Law Review, 2013

      • topic: Randomized study: full legal representation significantly improves outcomes vs limited assistance
      • relevance: Causal evidence that representation quality affects outcomes; if connected lawyers are an "upgrade," unequal access amplifies disparities

    • Acemoglu and Johnson. "Unbundling Institutions." JPE, 2005

      • topic: Property rights institutions vs contracting institutions have distinct effects on development
      • relevance: Favoritism degrades enforcement institutions, potentially affecting investment and firm behavior beyond individual cases

    • Ponticelli and Alencar. "Court Enforcement, Bank Loans, and Firm Investment." QJE, 2016

      • topic: Court congestion variation in Brazil; better enforcement amplifies gains from bankruptcy reform
      • relevance: Key Brazilian reference. If decisions are not neutral, enforcement quality is "priced" into firm behavior — direct welfare cost channel

    • Chemin. "Does Court Speed Shape Economic Activity?" JLEO, 2012

      • topic: Judicial speed reforms affect firm contracting and economic performance in India
      • relevance: Favoritism and unpredictability affect ex ante decisions (compliance, investment, contracting), not just who wins

    • Tyler. "Procedural Justice, Legitimacy, and the Effective Rule of Law." Crime and Justice, 2003

      • topic: Procedural fairness perception shapes legitimacy and cooperation with legal institutions
      • relevance: Key for legitimacy channel. Favoritism via family connections is especially corrosive because it undermines perceived impartiality

    • Feld and Frey. "Trust Breeds Trust: How Taxpayers are Treated." Economics of Governance, 2002

      • topic: Procedurally fair treatment increases trust and voluntary compliance
      • relevance: Formalizes legitimacy/trust as governance input; favoritism can reduce compliance via a social multiplier

    • Luttmer and Singhal. "Tax Morale." Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2014

      • topic: Non-pecuniary motivations for compliance (norms, trust, reciprocity, legitimacy)
      • relevance: Formal enforcement explains only part of compliance; favoritism may erode beliefs about justice with long-run consequences

    • Djankov et al

      • topic: Courts and contract enforcement
      • relevance: Theoretical framework for welfare effects of judicial impartiality

    • North and Weingast

      • topic: Institutions and credible commitment
      • relevance: Historical perspective on judicial impartiality

    • Dewatripont and Tirole (1999)

      • topic: Advocates — theory of adversarial system
      • relevance: Theoretical framework for positive vs negative externalities of lawyer specialization

    Market for professional connections

    • Blanes i Vidal, Draca, Fons-Rosen. "Revolving Door Lobbyists." AER, 2012

      • topic: Lobbyist revenue drops when connected politician loses power; substantial share of lobby value is access, not expertise
      • relevance: Key. Cleanest analog for "market for connections": connected lawyers sell probability of better outcome, and random judge assignment lets us distinguish access from skill

    • Bertrand, Bombardini, Trebbi. "Is It Whom You Know or What You Know?" AER, 2014

      • topic: Lobby is partly expertise, partly connections; methods to separate channels
      • relevance: Excellent reference for explicating tests that distinguish informational advantage from favoritism/corruption

    • Laband and Lentz. "Self-Recruitment in the Legal Profession." Journal of Labor Economics, 1992

      • topic: Intergenerational persistence in legal careers driven by skills transfer, knowledge, and network advantages
      • relevance: Direct support for dynastic entry argument: if judicial connections generate returns, families invest in occupational reproduction

    • Lo Bello and Morchio. "Like Father, Like Son: Occupational Choice, Intergenerational Persistence and Misallocation." Quantitative Economics, 2022

      • topic: Parental networks explain large share of occupational persistence; restricting networks affects allocation and welfare
      • relevance: Theoretical bridge between "connection as economic asset" and misallocation: networks directing talent toward rent-generating occupations can reduce efficiency

    Brazilian judiciary

    • Sadek (2006)

      • topic: Survey of Brazilian magistrates
      • relevance: 52% of active judges and 53.4% of retired judges reported having relatives in private legal practice

    • Lambais and Sigstad. "Judicial subversion: The effects of political power on court outcomes." Journal of Public Economics, 2023

      • topic: Winning mayoral candidates less likely to be convicted; effect stronger with fewer judges and less career stability
      • relevance: Political capture of Brazilian courts via relational channels; motivates that family/professional networks operate as parallel influence mechanism

    • Assumpcao and Trecenti. "Judicial Favoritism of Politicians: Evidence from Small Claims Court." arXiv, 2020

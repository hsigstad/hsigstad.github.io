Operação Anaconda

TRF-3 2003

One of Brazil's earliest major judicial corruption operations, targeting a criminal organization within the Federal Regional Court of the 3rd Region (São Paulo). Judges sold sentences and judicial information to benefit criminals, politicians, and businessmen.

Judge Rocha Mattos's ex-wife Norma Regina Cunha administered the organization's proceeds — R$550,000 cash found at her apartment. His sister (ex-auditor at Receita Federal) and her daughter (his niece, a PF delegate) suspected of aiding the scheme. Brothers Casem and Ali Mazloum were both federal judges at the same court.
US$19.4M repatriated from Switzerland
  • 12 people charged including 3 federal judges
  • Rocha Mattos convicted to 12 years, later 17 more for money laundering
  • Intermediary Wagner Rocha's office contained unsigned draft decisions from the judges

Individual Cases from CNJ Records

Beyond organized operations

The Intercept Brasil's analysis of CNJ disciplinary records identified 21 individual venda de sentenças cases since 2005 across 12 courts. Even among these smaller, individual cases, family members appear as intermediaries, money conduits, and beneficiaries:

  • TJ-BA (2012): Des. Rubem Dário's son (lawyer Nizan Gomes Cunha Neto) delivered R$400,000 bribe payment. The deal also included a job for the judge's son's girlfriend.
  • Rio Grande do Norte (2017): Judge José Dantas de Lira's son participated in scheme selling liminares for R$750 each. 22 fraudulent liminares over 6 months.
  • Pará: Judge José Admilson received R$70,000 for a habeas corpus, with money transferred via his sister's bank account to his brother's account.
  • TRT-MG: Des. Antônio Fernando Guimarães received a subsidized luxury apartment (R$200/month for a R$8,000/month property) from a law firm that received favorable rulings.
  • TRF-4: Des. Edgard Lippmann — the only magistrate with three separate compulsory retirements for selling decisions.
Source: The Intercept Brasil (2019) →

Recurring Patterns

Across these cases, the same structural features appear again and again — suggesting that judicial corruption in Brazil is not a matter of individual moral failure but of institutional design vulnerabilities.

Family as intermediary: Children, spouses, and in-laws serve as brokers, lobbyists, or front lawyers — providing plausible deniability while maintaining trust. Present in TJ-MS, TJ-GO, TJ-TO, TJ-MT, TST, STJ, and STF cases.
Cross-scheme design: Families avoid formal recusal triggers by routing favors through each other's courts, relatives, or colleagues. The impedimento system is systematically gamed.
Price escalation by proximity: The informal pricing table shows that family connections to judges directly increase the "cost" of a favorable decision, implying the market recognizes and prices the value of family ties.
Institutional protection: Legislatures have attempted to shield judges' children from investigation (364 votes). System "failures" in impedimento alerts conveniently go unpatched. Corrupt judges get promoted.
Geographic breadth: From Maranhão to São Paulo, from Tocantins to Mato Grosso do Sul — 14 tribunals now implicated. This is not a regional pathology but a national pattern with local variants.
Why this matters for our research
These cases collectively demonstrate that family connections between lawyers and judges are not merely a theoretical concern. The documented mechanisms — intermediation by children, cross-scheme routing, pricing by proximity — are precisely the channels our empirical identification strategy aims to quantify. The random assignment of cases to judges in Brazilian labor courts allows us to estimate the causal effect of these connections on case outcomes, moving beyond anecdote to systematic evidence.

Operação Sisamnes

Named after the Persian judge skinned alive for taking bribes. A sprawling scheme in which corrupt assessors inside STJ minister offices extracted draft decisions before publication, lobbyists brokered them to interested parties, and payments flowed through shell companies, gold bars, and luxury watches — all laundered through a lawyer wife's firm.

The 4-Step Pipeline

How draft decisions became commodities inside Brazil's highest appellate court

The 4-step pipeline: corrupt court staff extracted unpublished draft decisions, lobbyists brokered access to the drafts, payment flowed through shell companies and luxury goods, and a lawyer's firm provided the veneer of legitimacy.
How the draft extraction worked
Corrupt assessors and chiefs of staff inside STJ minister offices had access to the internal case management system. In some instances, a servidor would create, save, print, and delete a draft decision within minutes — leaving a digital trace but destroying the document itself. The printed copy was then passed to lobbyists who offered it to interested parties.

Why It Matters

What Sisamnes reveals about systemic corruption in Brazil's higher courts

Family as access channel
Hiring a minister's relative does not guarantee victory — as Andreson himself noted. But it provides privileged access: advance knowledge of draft decisions, favorable case distribution, and the implicit authority of a family name. The 2022 law that removed transparency requirements made these connections even harder to detect.
The pattern
Sisamnes reveals a multi-layered corruption infrastructure:
  • Institutional access: Corrupt servidores with system access extract unpublished decisions
  • Professional intermediaries: Lobbyists broker information and influence
  • Family networks: Ministers' relatives provide legitimacy and access
  • Financial infrastructure: Shell companies, luxury goods, and cash obscure payment trails
  • Legal cover: A lawyer spouse formally contracted by parties lends professional legitimacy
  • Scale: 11 of 33 gabinetes, 14 tribunals, R$2.8B in suspicious transactions

Leal (1977) -- Coronelismo: the municipality and representative government in Brazil

Leal (1977) -- Coronelismo: the municipality and representative government in Brazil

Classic study of Brazilian patronage politics (coronelismo). Originally published 1949. Analyzes the system of reciprocal exchange between local landowners (coroneis) and state government, where political loyalty is traded for control over local appointments -- including judicial and police posts.

Key findings relevant to connect project

  • The coronel is a rural landowner who commands "block votes" (votos de cabresto -- halter votes). Electoral strength gives him political prestige, which is the "natural culmination of his social and economic position as a landowner."
  • The coronel exercises de facto juridical power over dependents: "settling quarrels and disagreements, and sometimes handing down actual judgements which are respected by the interested parties."
  • The system operates through reciprocity at every level: the local boss delivers votes to the state government, which in return gives him control over local appointments and police.
  • Patronage produces systematic institutional dysfunction: "patronage plays a large part in the lack of organisation which is so marked in the administration in the municipalities."

Family and kinship as political infrastructure

  • Local political leaders are not always coroneis themselves. Higher education created "a class of doctors and lawyers whose relative distinction, combined with qualities of leadership and dedication, accustomed them to taking political charge." But crucially, "these same 'doctors' are either relations or kinsmen or political allies of the coroneis."
  • Leadership positions are inherited or consolidated through family networks. When coroneis become absentee (rising to state/federal positions), "lieutenants who remain on the spot themselves become the real local bosses" -- but defections among subordinates are common, making family ties more reliable than purely political ones.
  • State-level political leaders are connected to local bosses "through ties of family or friendship, through natural talent, by collusion or simply by force of circumstance."
  • The system of reciprocity operates through personal relationships at every rung: "pledges which are given between man and man in the municipality should be honoured."

Local power and the judiciary

  • The coronel's influence extends directly to judicial appointments. Among posts controlled by local bosses: "clerks of the court, the public prosecutor" and other court officials -- "for all such posts the candidates suggested or approved by the local boss are customarily the successful ones."
  • Historical accumulation of "administrative, police and jurisdictional functions, in the hands of the same authorities" -- blurring the line between executive and judicial power at the local level.
  • Justices of the peace were sometimes elected, sometimes nominated by state governments. During the Estado Novo (1937-1945), they were appointed by state governments directly.
  • Temporary judges who substitute for permanent ones create a channel for political influence: states could remove them before their 10-year tenure threshold or interrupt their service.
  • Despite formal prohibitions on judges participating in politics, "the continued existence of temporary judges, the machinery for promotion and removal, and the use of various methods of seduction and various types of pressure" mean that "it is still possible, especially in the interior, to meet partisan judges and prosecutors, not to mention junior officials who are almost always impassioned militants."

Key quotes

  • "These same 'doctors' are either relations or kinsmen or political allies of the coroneis." (Ch. 1) -- on lawyers/doctors as extensions of the coronel family network.
  • "For his friends, bread; for his enemies, a stick" (pao/pau) -- the logic of selective favoritism and persecution.
  • "The only crime is to lose" -- on how political victory retroactively legitimizes illegal acts.
  • "The local boss can often be found groping in the twilight zone between legality and illegality" -- on the structural ambiguity enabling patronage.
  • On partisan judges: "it is still possible, especially in the interior, to meet partisan judges and prosecutors, not to mention junior officials who are almost always impassioned militants."

Relevance to the connect project

  1. Historical roots of judge-lawyer family ties: Leal documents that lawyers and doctors who held local political power were typically relatives or kinsmen of the coroneis. This establishes a deep historical pattern where legal professionals and political elites are intertwined through family -- the same structural feature we measure in modern labor courts.
  2. Judicial appointments as patronage currency: Control over court clerks, prosecutors, and temporary judges was a key commodity in the exchange between local bosses and state government. This is the institutional ancestor of modern nepotism in the judiciary.
  3. Partisan judges as historical norm: The observation that judges in the interior were "impassioned militants" despite formal prohibitions suggests that judicial independence is a relatively recent and incomplete achievement in Brazil -- providing historical context for why family connections between lawyers and judges might still affect outcomes.
  4. "Bread for friends, stick for enemies": The coronelismo logic of differential treatment based on personal/family ties maps directly onto the mechanism we test -- whether connected lawyers receive favorable treatment from related judges.
  5. Reciprocity at every rung: The system of mutual obligation through personal ties that Leal describes at the political level parallels the network of favors between judges and lawyers that Mujica (2011) documents ethnographically in Peru.
  6. Persistence of informal institutions: Leal shows that formal legal reforms (constitutions of 1934, 1937, 1946) repeatedly failed to eliminate political influence over the judiciary. This supports the hypothesis that family-based connections persist as informal channels even within modern institutional frameworks like random case assignment.

Da Ros & Taylor (2017) -- Opening the Black Box: Three Decades of Reforms to Brazil's Judicial System

Comprehensive review of Brazilian judicial reforms from democratization through ~2017. Covers institutional design, accountability mechanisms, corruption, and the political economy of reform resistance. Co-authored with Matthew Taylor.

Key findings relevant to connect project

  • Family members of judges practice before the court, creating significant conflicts of interest. Wealthy defendants hire a judge's family member to join their defense team -- exactly the mechanism studied in the connect project.
  • Weak conflict-of-interest laws permit ethically challenging admixtures of judging, lecturing, consulting, and advocating, as well as "ample socializing between judges and defense lawyers" (Carvalho 2010a,b).
  • The 2004 reform (EC 45) explicitly sought to banish nepotism, yet the practice of hiring judges' relatives as lawyers persists in the lower courts.
  • Influence peddling is "dispersed across the less visible lower ranks of the Brazilian judiciary" -- consistent with the project's focus on trial-level labor courts rather than apex courts.

Accountability and internal control

  • The CNJ was created by the 2004 reform over strong opposition from judges and prosecutors to any constraints on their autonomy. Judges and prosecutors hold two-thirds of CNJ seats.
  • Between 1988 and 2013, out of 6,703 cases of potential judicial irregularities received by the CNJ, only 38 resulted in compulsory retirement (Franco 2015, 100-122).
  • In "all too many cases, the only possible punishment of corrupt judges in cases of suspected judicial corruption is mandatory retirement (with full pension!)."
  • First federal politician convicted and forced to serve court time was sentenced only in 2010.

Judicial lobbying against reform

  • "Well-organized and well-funded associations of judges" form a strong lobby that has pushed back against mandatory sentencing laws, arguing they conflict with "individuality in sentencing and thus unconstitutional."
  • Judges and prosecutors strongly opposed the creation of the CNJ and CNMP oversight bodies.
  • Even after binding precedent instruments were established in 2004, there remains "a strong bias against setting such precedents" -- preserving individual judicial discretion.

Institutional design and discretion

  • Random assignment at the STF: ~86% of STF decisions made individually by a single randomly assigned justice (the relator/rapporteur); only ~2% decided en banc, ~12% by panels.
  • A "breadth of legal justifications available to support a variety of judgments" gives judges latitude to decide based on "personal interests, political conceptions, or subjective theories of law and justice."
  • Hybrid constitutional review (concentrated + diffuse) is "perhaps the broadest institutional cause" of legal insecurity and unpredictable decisions.
  • The judiciary budget is 1.2% of GDP (2014), the highest of all federal systems in the Western hemisphere. Young federal judges earn 81% of an STF justice's salary -- strong salary autonomy insulates judges from external pressure.
  • Compensatory-only damages mean it is "often cheaper for consumer firms to hire an army of attorneys to respond to each case and delay decision-making, rather than to solve the actual problems" -- the deterrent effect of court decisions on illegal behavior is "close to nonexistent."

Judicial corruption

  • Operation Anaconda: corrupt sales of sentences by a federal judge in São Paulo.
  • TRT case: labor court judge stole >$90 million from construction of a regional labor court in São Paulo.
  • Four TST ministers (including the court president) received payments from Bradesco for lectures while presiding over >300 cases involving the bank, and did not recuse themselves.
  • An appellate judge in Bahia was accused of selling decisions to mayors in criminal corruption cases, aided by his son (Da Ros 2014, 285).

Key quotes

  • "Many judges' family members practice before the court, creating significant conflicts of interest: it is not unheard of for a wealthy defendant to simply hire a judge's family member to join their defense team."
  • "Weak laws governing conflicts of interest permit ethically challenging admixtures of judging, lecturing, consulting and advocating, as well as ample socializing between judges and defense lawyers."
  • "While this practice is dispersed across the less visible lower ranks of the Brazilian judiciary, it equally affects much more visible courts, such as the TST."
  • On CNJ opposition: judges and prosecutors dominated the resulting oversight bodies, "holding two-thirds of the seats."

Relevance to the connect project

  1. Direct description of the mechanism under study: Da Ros and Taylor explicitly document that defendants hire judges' family members as lawyers -- the core phenomenon the connect project measures and estimates causal effects for.
  2. Weak accountability explains persistence: The near-zero sanction rate (38/6,703 cases) and full-pension retirement as maximum punishment explain why family-connection favoritism persists despite formal anti-nepotism rules.
  3. Labor courts specifically implicated: The TRT corruption case and TST conflicts of interest show that the labor court system -- the project's setting -- has been a site of documented judicial corruption.
  4. Conflict-of-interest laws are weak: Carvalho (2010a,b) is cited as the source on weak recusal/conflict rules. This reference should be tracked down for the project's institutional background section.
  5. Reform resistance by judges: The organized judicial lobby against oversight and binding precedent helps explain why institutional safeguards against favoritism remain weak -- judges actively defend their discretion.
  6. Random assignment as institutional feature: The STF's use of random assignment of the relator parallels the random assignment in labor courts that the project exploits for identification.

Almeida (2010) -- A nobreza togada

Almeida (2010) -- A nobreza togada

Study of elite reproduction in the Brazilian legal field. Analyzes how a small set of elite law schools, family capital, and professional networks produce a self-reproducing "robed nobility" (nobreza togada) that dominates the judiciary, prosecution, and elite private practice.

Key findings relevant to connect project

  • The Brazilian legal elite is reproduced through a pipeline that combines elite schooling, family capital, and professional network formation -- all of which reinforce each other.
  • The same small set of institutions dominates across the judiciary, superior courts, prosecution, and elite private law firms.
  • 52.5% of active magistrates hold teaching positions, creating a dual judge-professor role that further concentrates elite networks.

Elite law schools and judiciary pipeline

São Paulo state courts

  • Among 42 of 111 judges at the Fórum Criminal Central de São Paulo: 33.3% from USP, 28.6% from PUC-SP.
  • Among 299 of 352 desembargadores at TJSP (2008): 48.4% from USP, 12.7% from PUC-SP, 7.6% from Mackenzie.
  • USP offers 460 annual places (2% of ~22,793 law places in São Paulo city). Combined with PUC-SP (550) and Mackenzie (560), these three schools account for only 6.8% of total places but dominate the judiciary.

Paraná

  • Of 71 desembargadores at TJPR: 36 from public institutions, 26 from Universidade Federal do Paraná alone.

Superior courts (STF, STJ, TST, CNJ)

  • STF (all 158 ministers since the Republic through 2008): 58.8% graduated from the two law schools founded in the imperial period (53 from São Paulo, 40 from Olinda/Recife). Only 4 ministers ever came from private institutions.
  • STJ: dominated by USP, UFBA, UFMG, UFRGS (3 ministers each).
  • TST (28 ministers, 2007): 21 from public institutions, 7 from private. UFMG and UnB lead with 3 each.
  • CNJ: again dominated by USP, UFMG, UFRGS.

85th MP-SP entrance exam (2006-2007)

  • Public institutions (18% of schools with approved graduates) produced 21% of 105 classified candidates. USP led with 10, followed by PUC-Campinas (9), PUC-SP (8), Mackenzie (6).

Elite private practice

  • The same schools dominate partners and associates at large law firms: USP (13.5% of partners, 7.2% of associates), PUC-SP (10.2% / 8.9%), Mackenzie (6.7% / 7.0%). Source: Análise Editorial (2007).

Family capital and social reproduction

  • Family investments in elite secondary education cannot be separated from the educational capital accumulated by the family across generations.
  • PUC-SP: 52% of law students come from families earning over 34 minimum wages. At Faculdade de Direito de Itu (low-prestige comparator): only 4.2%.
  • Students from lower social classes ("Classe C") face difficulty accessing prestigious internships due to lack of networking/relationships.
  • Elite law firms attract top students from elite schools through financial and symbolic returns (prestige of belonging to renowned firms, proximity to notable lawyers), enabling the formation of networks that produce high quantities of social capital.
  • Curricular references to passages through traditional public secondary schools (colégios públicos tradicionais) are not rare among legal elites -- indicating a specific tracked pipeline.

The judiciary-academia nexus

  • 52.5% of active magistrates dedicate time to teaching -- a remarkably high rate that creates a dual professional identity.
  • At the STF (11 ministers at time of study): 9 hold stricto sensu postgraduate degrees (combined with lato sensu in at least 4 cases); 10 have taught at university level, mostly at public and Catholic institutions.
  • Similar profile at STJ and TST. At STJ, 32.3% of ministers hold master's and/or doctoral degrees.
  • The opposition to Minister Toffoli's nomination to the STF tried to associate "notório saber jurídico" (notable legal knowledge) with holding a postgraduate degree -- which Toffoli lacked.

The "Escola Processual Paulista" and legislative influence

  • A concentrated group of procedural law specialists at USP's Departamento de Direito Processual has had privileged participation in commissions responsible for drafting central procedural reforms since redemocratization.
  • This group, known as the Escola Processual Paulista, has intellectual ties to Italian processualists.
  • The Instituto Brasileiro de Direito Processual Civil (IBDP), founded 1958, institutionalized this group.
  • Joaquim Falcão (FGV Direito Rio) is identified as one of the principal intellectuals of the Reforma do Judiciário.

The "nobreza togada" concept

  • The term captures a self-reproducing elite that combines:
    • Educational capital (elite law schools)
    • Family capital (intergenerational accumulation)
    • Social capital (networks formed during school and early career)
    • Symbolic capital (prestige of elite institutions and positions)
  • This elite straddles the judiciary, academia, prosecution, and elite private practice, with individuals often occupying positions in multiple fields simultaneously.

Relevance to the connect project

  1. Elite reproduction explains the density of family ties: The project documents family connections between judges and lawyers. Almeida shows why these connections are so prevalent -- legal careers in Brazil are reproduced through family capital and elite school pipelines that concentrate relatives in the same professional field.
  2. Same schools, same networks: The extreme concentration of judges and elite lawyers in a handful of institutions (USP, PUC-SP, Mackenzie, federal universities) means that family connections often overlap with school connections, reinforcing trust networks.
  3. The judge-professor dual role: With 52.5% of magistrates teaching, judges are embedded in academic networks that further connect them to lawyers and future lawyers -- an additional channel for the connections measured in the project.
  4. Social capital as mechanism: Almeida's framework suggests that family connections operate not just through direct favoritism but through the accumulated social capital that makes certain lawyers more effective at navigating the judicial system -- consistent with both corrupt and non-corrupt channels in the project's mechanism taxonomy.
  5. Institutional context for Brazil: Provides the sociological backdrop for why Brazilian courts have the family connection patterns observed in the data, grounding the empirical findings in the structure of elite reproduction.

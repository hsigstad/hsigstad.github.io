Mujica (2011) -- Micropolíticas de la corrupción

Ethnographic study of corruption networks in Lima's Palace of Justice (Peru). Based on fieldwork inside the judicial bureaucracy. Maps the micro-level mechanics of how bribes flow, who can approach whom, and why family/university ties are the essential infrastructure.

Key findings relevant to connect project

  • Corruption in the judiciary is not organized as a mafia, cartel, or clientelist hierarchy. It operates through fluid, overlapping networks with no stable boss or center of operations.
  • There is no single "principal agent" -- it is a network of actors and institutions functioning simultaneously.
  • Many participants engage in corrupt acts not for immediate gain but to become known within the institution's networks, which allows them to remain in the system.
  • Networks are unstable, with high personnel turnover, but operate through latent channels that persist even as individuals rotate.
  • New actors are integrated with great caution.

Trust and family as corruption infrastructure

  • "Solo sobornar a amigos" -- you only bribe friends. Approaching someone without a prior relationship is too risky.
  • Friends (amigos) are an essential form of capital for navigating the system.
  • Lawyers depend on university friends or family members to access the corruption network.
  • Judges obtain their positions thanks to contacts from university or family ("todos tienen padrinos" -- everyone has patrons).
  • Padrinazgo (patronage) is important, but there is no stable hierarchy with a fixed head, due to high rotation and instability.
  • You do not share your contact with others -- intermediaries want to preserve their role as go-betweens.
  • The network must be actively used or it dissolves.

The "trueque" to circumvent nepotism law

  • Judges exchange favors to place each other's relatives without technically violating anti-nepotism rules: "trueque para no transgredir ley de nepotismo" -- a swap/exchange to avoid transgressing the nepotism law.
  • This maps directly to the esquema cruzado (cross-placement scheme) documented in Brazilian courts: Judge A hires Judge B's relative, and vice versa.
  • The same trueque mechanism is used for promotions.

Network structure

  • Networks span multiple layers of the judicial bureaucracy:
    • External networks: document forgers, informal lawyers, "abogados al paso"
    • Intermediation networks: security guards (guachimanes), police, litigating lawyers, system users
    • Outer bureaucracy: mesa de partes (filing office) staff, archive personnel
    • Middle bureaucracy: court secretaries (secretarias de juzgado), relatores and their staff
    • Upper bureaucracy / deliberation: judges, vocales (appellate judges), and their trusted staff
  • Security guards (vigilantes) function as network hubs.
  • Mesa de partes is critical for accepting forged documents and making documents disappear. More important documents cost more to "lose."
  • Bribes are often paid at lunch.

Everyone knows, but protection through networks

  • "Todos saben y nadie sabe" -- everyone knows and nobody knows. People are roughly aware of who accepts bribes, but they use their networks for mutual protection.
  • The oversight body (OCMA) is itself part of the network -- its members are friends with the judges they oversee.
  • "A mis amigos todo, a mis enemigos la ley" -- for my friends everything, for my enemies the law. OCMA selectively enforces.
  • OCMA only controls judges, not lower functionaries.
  • There is no one to control the controllers.
  • Judges who accept too many bribes are expelled from the system (excessive risk-taking is punished by the network itself).

Differences by rank

  • Vocales (appellate judges) accept fewer direct bribes than lower-court judges; they rely more on padrinazgo (patronage) than cash.
  • Higher-ranking officials engage in less obvious, more indirect forms of corruption.
  • Two distinct types of corruption: meddling with evidence vs. meddling with judgment.

Relevance to the connect project

  1. Family ties as enabling infrastructure: The ethnography confirms that family connections are not incidental but structurally necessary for judicial corruption to function. Lawyers need family or university ties to access the bribery network.
  2. Esquema cruzado / trueque: Direct evidence of cross-placement schemes to circumvent nepotism laws, exactly the mechanism hypothesized in the Brazilian labor courts.
  3. Trust requirement: Random assignment of cases to judges is insufficient to prevent corruption if the lawyer already has a trust-based relationship with the judge through family or university ties. The connection pre-exists the case assignment.
  4. Network resilience: Even with personnel turnover, the latent channels persist -- suggesting that measuring connections at a single point in time may capture durable relationships.
  5. Rank-dependent mechanisms: Higher-ranking judges use more indirect channels, which may explain heterogeneity by court level.
  6. Cross-country evidence: Peru provides a Latin American comparator with similar institutional features (civil law tradition, career judiciary, anti-nepotism rules that are circumvented).
